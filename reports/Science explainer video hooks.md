# Top Science Explainers Show Before They Tell

The best science, tech and math explainers almost never "hook" with a trick. Instead they **put the video's central object, claim or question on screen and in words within 0–10 seconds**, and usually within the first sentence. A pixelated handwritten 3 ("This is a 3."), a water computer firing ("There it goes. — Yay!"), "There is a hole at the bottom of math": none of these openings uses a channel intro or logo. Across 39 high-view openings read directly from YouTube caption tracks, **seven recurring structures cover nearly all of them**, and most hooks stack two or three within 30 seconds: "This is X", payoff first, a flat impossible claim, the familiar-then-"but" reversal, a quiz question, a specific number, and a personal confession. The analyst frameworks (MrBeast's production document, Kallaway's "triple hook", Jenny Hoyos's mute test, vidIQ's templates) agree on one job for the opening: **prove the title and thumbnail promise fast, with spoken words, on-screen text and visuals all pointing at the same thing.** The retention data supports this. YouTube's own "Intro" metric is the share of viewers still watching at 30 seconds. On a well-measured channel, good intros hold about **60–68%** at 30 s and weak ones fall below 40%, and most of the loss happens in the first few seconds. On feeds the decision window is 1–3 seconds. For a layman audience watching a technical project, the practical rule is: open on the most concrete, visible artifact of the project, say in under 15 words why it should be impossible or surprising, and prove it on screen before second 10.

Two limits apply to this evidence. First, it is a sample of successes. High-view videos that open a certain way do not prove the opening caused the views, and the low-view videos that opened the same way are not in the set. Second, the on-screen descriptions come from the transcript's pointing words ("this", "here", "There it goes") or from memory, not frame-by-frame inspection. Openings for Steve Mould's "The Spring Paradox" and Stand-up Maths' domino computer could not be retrieved, and only one Sebastian Lague video was sampled.

## Seven patterns form the working hook library

Every template below is built from the openings above. Where analysts name a matching formula, it is mapped on. Most of the named formulas circulating online are secondhand: they come from vendor blogs, AI transcript summaries and course listings. The most reliable verbatim templates come from a single vidIQ guide ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-intros/)). The research found **no verified viral examples** for several popular creator-copy formulas, including "this shouldn't be possible", "nobody talks about this" and "This is X. Nothing special. Except…". Those formulas appear below only where a real opening does the same work.

### Pattern 1: "This is X", the object-first opening

This is the most common single construction. It appears in **at least 9 of the 36 spoken openings**, and it dominates hardware and visual-math topics. **Template:** "This is [concrete thing]. [One sentence on why it's ordinary or striking]. But [the twist or stakes]." **Real examples:** "This is a 3" ([3B1B](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk)); "This is an activation atlas" ([Welch Labs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UZDiGooFs54)); "this little guy I have here is a transistor" ([Ben Eater](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sTu3LwpF6XI)); "This is a bird feeder, and everything to my left is my attempt at making it squirrel-proof" ([Mark Rober](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hFZFjoX2cGg)); "This appears blue… But that doesn't change the fact that color is an illusion" ([Vsauce](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=evQsOFQju08)). **Visual pairing:** the object fills the frame at second 0, and the word "this" is literally a pointer at it. **When it works:** whenever you have a single, clean image of the subject. It is weakest when the object looks boring and the twist arrives late.

### Pattern 2: payoff first, then rewind

**Template:** "[Show the finished result working — no explanation]." then "[How did we get here?] To understand that, we need to go back [time/step]." **Real examples:** Steve Mould's "There it goes. — Yay!" before any explanation of the water computer ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IxXaizglscw)); 3B1B's "This right here is what we're going to build to" over the finished Fourier animation ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=spUNpyF58BY)); Rober's glitter-bomb rewind ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xoxhDk-hwuo)); sammyuri's 30+ seconds of wordless Minecraft-in-Minecraft footage (7.65M views) ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-BP7DhHTU-I)). The analyst version is vidIQ's in-medias-res template, "It's [time]. I'm [in the middle of problem]. Here's what I did next" ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-intros/)), and Hoyos's rule that the payoff should be visible in frame one ([vidIQ on Jenny Hoyos](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-jenny-hoyos-gets-10m-views-per-youtube-short/)). **When it works:** build and simulation videos, where "it actually works" is the promise. It is the default for anything built from unlikely parts.

### Pattern 3: the impossible claim, stated flatly

**Template:** "There is [a thing] that [violates an expectation]. [Restate the consequence in plain words]." **Real examples:** "There is a hole at the bottom of math… There will always be true statements that cannot be proven" (30.8M) ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HeQX2HjkcNo)); "it still seems wrong even if you know the answer" ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iSNsgj1OCLA)); "color is an illusion" ([Vsauce](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=evQsOFQju08)). This is the real-world version of the unverified "this shouldn't be possible" formula. The matching analyst template is vidIQ's curiosity hook: "I thought I knew [topic], until I found out [surprising twist]" ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-intros/)). **Risk:** this pattern carries the highest mismatch risk. vidIQ warns that contrarian hooks need "logic and proof behind" them ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-intros/)), and Kallaway's "lock-in zone" demands proof in the next 5–10 s ([SozAI transcript](https://sozai.app/transcript/psychology-killer-hooks/)).

### Pattern 4: familiar thing, then "but" or "and yet"

**Template:** "[Something everyone accepts]. But / and yet [the reversal]." A variant is "This may look like [X], but it's actually [Y]." Another is "You shouldn't be impressed by [A]. You should be impressed by [B]." **Real examples:** Kurzgesagt's ant ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=16W7c0mb-rE)) and AI ("Humans rule Earth without competition. But…") ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fa8k8IQ1_X0)); Veritasium's "But today, a perfect storm…" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GVsUOuSjvcg)); Branch's "it's actually composed of 2.1 million vertices" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C8YtdC8mxTU)); From Scratch's not-A-but-B ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zXPiqk0-zDY)). The same but/so mechanism is what Hoyos chains through a whole Short, with conflict, resolution and new conflict linked by "but" and "so" ([vidIQ on Jenny Hoyos](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-jenny-hoyos-gets-10m-views-per-youtube-short/)). **When it works:** a layman already holds the first half, so this pattern needs no prerequisite knowledge.

### Pattern 5: the question or quiz

**Template:** "Imagine [concrete setup]. [Question]. Is it [A], [B], or [C]?" A variant is "What if [assumption] is wrong, and the real reason is [new idea]?" ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-intros/)). **Real examples:** Veritasium's 300,000 km circuit with its multiple-choice options ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHIhgxav9LY)); "What if they all had the same solution?" ([Veritasium AlphaFold](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P_fHJIYENdI)); "what if we just start with a microprocessor?" ([Ben Eater](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LnzuMJLZRdU)); "How can a bunch of stupid things do smart things together?" ([Kurzgesagt](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=16W7c0mb-rE)). Commentary on Veritasium describes the channel as starting "with the misconception" so the explanation becomes the reward ([gist.ly](https://gist.ly/youtube-summarizer/the-secrets-behind-veritasiums-viral-success)). That source is secondary but consistent with the openings. **When it works:** when the viewer can form a confident wrong guess. A quiz makes the viewer commit, and the commitment is what holds them.

### Pattern 6: the specific number

**Template:** "[Thing] contains / took / costs [precise number] [unit]." **Real examples:** "37 interlocking bronze gears" ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgF3OX8nT0w)); "600 hours and five months" ([From Scratch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zXPiqk0-zDY)); "28x28 pixels" ([3B1B](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk)); "billions of nanoscopic transistors" ([Branch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=16zrEPOsIcI)); the packaging example "Why Are 96,000,000 Black Balls on This Reservoir?" cited by Colin and Samir ([Colin and Samir](https://www.colinandsamir.com/resources/the-new-rules-of-youtube-from-paddy-galloway)). Kallaway's text-hook rules call for specific numbers ([SozAI](https://sozai.app/transcript/psychology-killer-hooks/)). **When it works:** almost always as a modifier. The number rarely carries a hook alone; it makes patterns 1–4 credible.

### Pattern 7: the personal stake or confession

**Template:** "I [admit something / have always wondered / spent N hours on] [X]." **Real examples:** "Can I tell you something I'm bad at? I am terrible at making clickbait" ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S2xHZPH5Sng)); Lague's "it's always been such a mysterious thing to me" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QZwneRb-zqA)); mattbatwings' "Warning: this video is nerdy, like really nerdy… my most requested build" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CW9N6kGbu2I)). Hoyos's "I never do THIS, and here's why…" is the short-form cousin ([vidIQ on Jenny Hoyos](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-jenny-hoyos-gets-10m-views-per-youtube-short/)). **When it works:** for a solo builder whose curiosity is the story. It works best stacked with a payoff (pattern 2), because confession alone relies on the audience already liking the narrator.

Two modifiers recur across all seven patterns. The first is **second-person implication**: "your brain has no trouble…" ([3B1B](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk)), "every programmer has to deal with time zones" ([Computerphile](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-5wpm-gesOY)). The second is **discrediting prior explanations**: "I find all of them either incorrect or incomplete" ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iSNsgj1OCLA)). Sponsors never come before the hook. Welch Labs reads its sponsor at about 32 s, after the hook ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UZDiGooFs54)), and Veritasium's electricity video uses only a one-line tag at 0 s ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHIhgxav9LY)).

The best long-form hooks also run a short, predictable sequence. The 3B1B neural-network opening is the clearest model. It shows a trivially easy thing ("This is a 3"), then has the viewer "appreciate how crazy it is that brains can do this so effortlessly." Next it flips the task "from comically trivial to dauntingly difficult," then states the stakes and promises scope, all within about 85 seconds ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk)). One practitioner guide formalises the same structure: the first 10 seconds "prove the title is true with one real example," the next 10 say why it matters, and the last 10 preview the video ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-hooks-and-retention/)). Kallaway describes the same rhythm for short-form: "a hook, there's a dance… context and conflict… set up, rehook. Set up, rehook" ([Castmagic](https://www.castmagic.io/podcast/rhythm-hooks-and-a-billion-views-kane-kallaways-short-form-video-content-secrets)).

## The title makes the promise; the first sentence must pay it

The single most consistent finding is that **the first spoken sentence confirms or restates the title within 0–12 seconds**, often almost word for word. "The Riddle That Seems Impossible Even If You Know The Answer" opens with "There is a riddle that is so counterintuitive, it still seems wrong even if you know the answer" ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iSNsgj1OCLA)). "Math's Fundamental Flaw" becomes "There is a hole at the bottom of math" at 1 s ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HeQX2HjkcNo)). "I Made A Water Computer And It Actually Works" gets its proof at 3 s ([Steve Mould](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IxXaizglscw)). Kurzgesagt says "our last invention" at 7 s under the title "Humanity's Final Invention?" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fa8k8IQ1_X0)). Some big channels have also retitled older videos so the title now carries the hook's central image. CGP Grey's automation video is now titled "What Happened to Horses Is Happening to Us" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Pq-S557XQU)).

The analysts agree with what the videos show. The leaked MrBeast production document, which two former producers confirmed as legitimate ([Passionfruit](https://passionfru.it/mrbeast-leaked-document-81151/)), calls the first minute "the most important minute of each video". In Simon Willison's summary, the first minute "demonstrates that their expectations from the thumbnail will be met," and "If a viewer feels their expectations are not being matched, they'll click away" ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/15/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)). YouTube's own Help Center makes the same diagnosis. A high Intro score may mean the opening matched the title and thumbnail, and its **first suggested fix for a low score is to change the title and thumbnail to better reflect the video**, not to re-edit ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)). Derek Muller shows how much packaging decides who arrives at all. His basketball-from-a-dam video got "16.3 million views, but almost none of those views came on the YouTube platform. Why? Because I gave it this thumbnail" ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S2xHZPH5Sng)).

Withholding the answer works only under specific conditions. Veritasium's AlphaFold hook escalates the title but holds back the word "AlphaFold" for 30+ seconds, which creates a deliberate curiosity gap ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P_fHJIYENdI)). Its "Game of Life" title pays off obliquely, opening on "Some things are not normal" and power laws rather than Conway's automaton ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HBluLfX2F_k)). Both work because the subject itself is delivered at once, even when the name is held back. The rule is to **delay the label, never the subject**.

## Retention is decided in seconds, then flattens

**Long-form YouTube.** YouTube's "Intro" metric "tells you what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds," and YouTube publishes no benchmark for it ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)). The best-documented numbers come from vidIQ's own channel over the 12 months to June 2026. Strong intros held **59.9–67.9%** at 30 s, while one weak video held only **38.1%** despite 1.95M views. On one video the curve fell from about 95% in the opening seconds to about 65% at 30 s and then levelled off. vidIQ's target is to keep at least 60% past 30 s, with the strongest hooks above 65% ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/increase-audience-retention-youtube/)). The causes vidIQ names for its weak intros are slow or branded cold opens, a hook that doesn't match the title or thumbnail, no clear value promise within about 15 s, and slow pacing ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/increase-audience-retention-youtube/)). The MrBeast document treats losing **21M of 60M** clickers (about 35%) in the first minute as "a reasonably good result" ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/15/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)). Retention Rabbit's widely repeated 2025 figures (23.7% average retention, 55%+ lost in the first 60 s, and intros above 65% correlating with 58% higher average view duration) could not be verified against the original report and should be treated as indicative only ([Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/what-is-good-youtube-audience-retention)). Third-party benchmarks disagree: one calls 70%+ at 30 s "solid" and 80%+ "exceptional" ([Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/what-is-good-youtube-audience-retention)), while another says 30–40% leave within 30 s ([Boss Wallah](https://bosswallah.com/blog/creator-hub/youtube-retention-in-the-first-30-seconds-how-views-are-won-or-lost/)). The two ranges overlap, which suggests **about 60–70% retained at 30 s is a realistic target for a 5–15 minute science explainer, and below about 50% signals a mismatch or a slow start.** Do not mix this 30-second figure with MrBeast's first-minute figure; they measure different windows.

**Shorts, Reels, TikTok.** No platform publishes an organic 3-second hold benchmark. YouTube Shorts reports "Viewed vs. swiped away" ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/the-creators-guide-to-youtube-shorts-analytics)). Vendors call 70–80% viewed good and say struggling channels sit at 50–60% ([Joyspace](https://joyspace.ai/youtube-shorts-analytics-metrics-growth); [Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/p/youtube-shorts-analytics-metrics-viral)), but one real channel's 90-day figure was 26.6% viewed ([Social Media Examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/how-to-analyze-your-youtube-shorts/)). Since 31 March 2025 a Shorts "view" counts on every start or replay with no minimum watch time, and the old definition became "engaged views" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/26/youtube-is-changing-how-youtube-shorts-views-are-counted)). **Raw Shorts view counts therefore no longer measure the hook; viewed-vs-swiped and engaged views do.** The only platform-published attention funnel is Facebook's from 2016. Feed content gets **1.7 s** on mobile, and of people who watch 3 s of a video, **65% reach 10 s and 45% reach 30 s** ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-releases-new-guide-to-help-advertisers-maximize-video-ad-performan/450818)). The often-quoted "1.7 seconds for Reels" traces back to this feed figure, not to Reels research ([Socialinsider](https://socialinsider.io/blog/instagram-reels-algorithm/)). TikTok's official creative guidance says "90% of ad recall impact is captured within the first six seconds" and that "faster scene changes typically draw viewers in early" ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/creative-best-practices-top-performing-ads)). Instagram's head says Reels ranking weighs watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach ([Dataslayer](https://www.dataslayer.ai/blog/instagram-algorithm-2025-complete-guide-for-marketers)). Creator guidance compresses these windows further. Jenny Hoyos says "you have one second to hook someone, especially on Shorts" ([Demand Curve](https://www.demandcurve.com/newsletters/growth-newsletter-187)) and treats the hook as about 3 s ([vidIQ on Jenny Hoyos](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-jenny-hoyos-gets-10m-views-per-youtube-short/)). Kallaway sets a 3–5 s hook followed by a 5–10 s "lock-in zone" that must confirm the claim with proof ([SozAI](https://sozai.app/transcript/psychology-killer-hooks/)).

Combined, the sources give a layered timeline. **0–1 s: a visual stop. 1–3 s: subject plus stakes. 3–10 s: proof. 10–30 s (long-form): why it matters and what the video will deliver.** Both the Facebook funnel and vidIQ's curve show that **attrition slows sharply once the first few seconds are won**. That is why effort spent on the first sentence pays more than effort spent anywhere else in the script.

**Educational and faceless content.** The strongest academic evidence is still Guo, Kim and Rubin's analysis of **6.9M edX video sessions**. Median engagement was "at most 6 minutes, regardless of total video length." Khan-style hand-drawn tutorials beat slide or code screencasts, and the authors credit the "natural motion of human handwriting." Engagement rose up to 2x with faster, enthusiastic speech, and even 254 words per minute stayed understandable when the information was also shown visually ([Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)). Ad research measured every 250 ms found that **surprise momentarily reduces zapping, while joy that builds over time sustains viewing** ([Tilburg University](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/emotion-induced-engagement-in-internet-video-ads/)). That maps neatly onto hook-then-payoff: use surprise to stop the scroll and escalating delight to hold the viewer. These studies are older and partly from ad or MOOC contexts, so they show direction, not precise effect sizes, for creator videos.

## Spoken, text and visual hooks must point at one thing

In every catalogued opening, **the visual hook is the subject itself, shown at once**. It is a physical object (a transistor, the 6502 chip, the Antikythera mechanism, a patch-panel analog computer), a finished build already running (the water computer, Pong inside Terraria), or one striking visualization (an activation atlas, an RDR2 scene dissolving into 3.5 million triangles). The narration points straight at it with "this", "here" and "There it goes" ([Ben Eater](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LnzuMJLZRdU); [Steve Mould](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IxXaizglscw); [Branch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C8YtdC8mxTU)). Abstract topics get a single concrete stand-in: a pixelated 3 for machine learning, one ant for emergence, a battery and bulb with 300,000 km wires for electromagnetism ([3B1B](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk); [Kurzgesagt](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=16W7c0mb-rE); [Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHIhgxav9LY)). Primer shows that a strong enough visual can carry an almost empty sentence. "This video is about natural selection" works because the blob creatures are already on screen ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0ZGbIKd0XrM)).

Kallaway's **"triple hook"** is the most detailed alignment framework found. The **text hook** is on-screen words, explicitly "not the captions," and he calls it the most important part. The **visual hook** is what is shown beyond the words. The **spoken hook** is the line said aloud. A viewer should be able to guess the other two from any one, and misalignment is a main cause of failure. His text-hook rules are a subject term plus a pain point, dream outcome or "future-state change"; specific numbers; at most two lines broken at natural phrases; no punctuation or transition words; and about 3 s on screen, placed where the eye lands first. His spoken-hook rules are that speed means compression (subject plus stakes in the fewest words), not talking faster, and clarity means only one possible reading, with no wordplay ([SozAI transcript](https://sozai.app/transcript/psychology-killer-hooks/)). This source is an automated transcript summary, so treat the details as approximate. Hoyos's version is a single test: the hook "needs to be so good that you can be watching the video on mute and still know what it's about" ([vidIQ on Jenny Hoyos](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-jenny-hoyos-gets-10m-views-per-youtube-short/)). Platform data backs designing for silence. Facebook recommended putting the key message up front without relying on audio ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-releases-new-guide-to-help-advertisers-maximize-video-ad-performan/450818)). In a self-reported survey, 69% of people watch with sound off in public and 80% say captions make them more likely to finish ([MarketingCharts](https://www.marketingcharts.com/digital/video-108656/page/2?et_blog)). Even so, 88% of TikTok users call sound vital ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/creative-best-practices-top-performing-ads)). The answer is burned-in key text plus an energetic voice, not one or the other.

The failure modes are well sourced. The first is **pointless spectacle**: vidIQ's rule is "weird is fine, but pointless is fatal" ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-intros/)), and Kallaway warns that overdone visual gimmicks look cheap ([SozAI](https://sozai.app/transcript/psychology-killer-hooks/)). The second is **a weak premise dressed up**: the MrBeast document contrasts "'I Spent 50 Hours In My Front Yard' is lame" with the far stronger image of someone sitting in a ketchup-filled bathtub ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/15/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)). The third is **slow pacing carried over from long-form into feeds**: Kallaway notes that slow podcast clips fail on TikTok and Instagram ([Castmagic](https://www.castmagic.io/podcast/rhythm-hooks-and-a-billion-views-kane-kallaways-short-form-video-content-secrets)). For faceless animated explainers like 3B1B's, the visual state change takes the place of the talking-head cut. A new diagram, a zoom or a stroke drawing itself on gives the same "natural motion" Guo et al. found engaging ([Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)).

## Twelve rules for hooking laymen on a technical project

The rules below combine the evidence above into a checklist for writing the opening of a video about a technical build, such as a small neural network that learns to add, or a computer built from logic gates. Each rule is tied to its strongest support.

| # | Rule | Why (evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Open on the artifact, not the topic.** Frame 0 shows the thing itself: the model doing the task, the circuit lighting up, the finished output. | Every catalogued visual hook is the subject shown at once ([Steve Mould](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IxXaizglscw); [Welch Labs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UZDiGooFs54)) |
| 2 | **First sentence under 15 words; content by second 4.** No greeting, logo or "in this video". | More than half of openings are under 15 words; median first content at 0–4 s; branded cold opens are a named cause of weak intros ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/increase-audience-retention-youtube/)) |
| 3 | **Say the title out loud, in plain words, by second 10.** | Title restated at 0–12 s across the sample ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iSNsgj1OCLA)); expectation mismatch drives click-away ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/15/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)) |
| 4 | **Start from what the layman already believes, then turn it with "but".** | The familiar-then-reversal pattern needs no prerequisites ([Kurzgesagt](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=16W7c0mb-rE); [3B1B](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk)) |
| 5 | **Stack two or three patterns, never one.** "This is X" + number + "but" is the workhorse. | Most catalogued hooks stack 2–3 structures in 30 s ([From Scratch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zXPiqk0-zDY)) |
| 6 | **Use one exact number** for effort, scale or size. | "37 gears", "600 hours", "28x28 pixels" ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgF3OX8nT0w)); Kallaway's specificity rule ([SozAI](https://sozai.app/transcript/psychology-killer-hooks/)) |
| 7 | **Prove any bold claim on screen before second 10.** | Kallaway's lock-in zone; vidIQ's "logic and proof" warning ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-intros/)) |
| 8 | **Delay the jargon label, never the subject.** Name "transformer", "XOR" or "carry" only after the viewer has seen what it does. | AlphaFold withholds its name for 30+ s while showing the stakes ([Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P_fHJIYENdI)); 3B1B unpacks "GPT" only after the hook ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wjZofJX0v4M)) |
| 9 | **Pass the mute test.** On-screen text of at most 2 lines, about 3 s, matching the spoken line and the image. | Hoyos's mute rule ([vidIQ on Jenny Hoyos](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-jenny-hoyos-gets-10m-views-per-youtube-short/)); triple-hook alignment ([SozAI](https://sozai.app/transcript/psychology-killer-hooks/)) |
| 10 | **Point at the screen with words.** Use "this", "here", "watch". Every sentence in the hook should have a visible referent. | Deictic narration across 3B1B, Ben Eater, Mould ([Ben Eater](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sTu3LwpF6XI)) |
| 11 | **Keep energy and motion high.** Fast, enthusiastic narration and drawn-on animation, not static text. | Engagement rises with speaking rate; handwriting motion beats static fonts ([Guo, Kim & Rubin](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)); faster scene changes help early ([TikTok](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/creative-best-practices-top-performing-ads)) |
| 12 | **Re-hook every 10–20 seconds** with a new but/so question; put sponsors after the hook. | Kallaway's "set up, rehook" ([Castmagic](https://www.castmagic.io/podcast/rhythm-hooks-and-a-billion-views-kane-kallaways-short-form-video-content-secrets)); Welch Labs' sponsor at about 32 s ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UZDiGooFs54)) |

Applied to a project like a tiny transformer that learns to add, or a CPU built from learned gates, the patterns produce openings like the ones below. They are illustrative drafts, not sourced examples. Every claim in them has to match what the project actually shows. A **"This is X" + "but"** draft (after 3B1B): "This is a tiny AI adding two numbers. A child can do this. But nobody told this network what 'carry the one' means — and it invented it anyway." A **payoff-first** draft (after Mould and From Scratch): open on the model's output scrolling correctly, then "You shouldn't be impressed that it adds. You should be impressed that, inside, it built the same logic gate engineers use in every computer chip." A **quiz** draft (after Veritasium): "A network with one layer learns to add. Where does it store the carry — in attention, in its neurons, or nowhere at all?" The on-screen text for the first draft would be two short lines such as "AI invented 'carry the one'", over the network visibly lighting up when the sum crosses ten. The test for each draft is the same: a viewer with the sound off should be able to tell what the video is about from the frame and the text alone, and the claim should be visibly proved before the tenth second.

## Conclusion

The research changes what a "hook" means for this genre. The named formulas sold online are mostly repackaged versions of a few moves the top explainers make without fanfare, and several of the most-hyped templates have no verified viral example at all. What actually distinguishes 3Blue1Brown, Veritasium, Kurzgesagt, Mould, Primer and Welch Labs is not clever phrasing. It is the discipline of **making the abstract visible at frame zero and keeping the title's promise in the first sentence**. For a technical project this is good news. The deepest result in a project is usually one concrete, showable moment: the network getting a sum right, a neuron firing exactly at the carry. The hook is finding that moment and saying, in fewer than 15 words, why a layman should not expect it to be true.

The numbers also point to where effort pays. Retention curves fall steeply in the first seconds and then flatten, YouTube treats a low intro score first as a packaging problem, and Shorts view counts no longer measure the hook at all. So the title, thumbnail, first frame and first sentence should be designed together as one unit and measured with the 30-second Intro figure and the viewed-vs-swiped rate, not raw views. Two things remain unknown: no public data isolates which of the seven patterns causes better retention, and no platform publishes organic 3-second benchmarks. A creator's own A/B tests of the first ten seconds will be the most reliable evidence available.
