# Capacity allocation and skill interference in small language models: does learning arithmetic cost language, and does an offloaded skill decay?

Setting this note is written for: a ~1.4M-weight GPT, context 64, ~7,000 steps on CPU, TinyStories-style text mixed with add/subtract/multiply word problems (results < 256). Variant A emits a calculator call and never computes; variant B writes the answer itself. Observed: story perplexity 15.66 (tool) vs 15.33 (no tool) at equal steps, i.e. the tool model is slightly *worse* on stories, by ~0.02 nats.

Scale flags used throughout: [≤10M] = evidence from models comparable to the user's; [100M–1B] = mid-scale; [≥1B] = billion-parameter models, extrapolation to 1.4M is unsupported.

## Key question 1: Knowledge/skill capacity per parameter (Allen-Zhu & Li 2024 and related) and what it implies for 1.4M params at 7,000 steps

### Takeaway
Factual-knowledge capacity is ~2 bits/parameter when each fact is seen ~1,000 times and ~1 bit/parameter at ~100 exposures, measured on GPT-2-style models from about 1M to 0.5B parameters, so the law was tested down to the user's size. A naive "memorize every arithmetic fact under 256" lookup table would cost on the order of 20–40% of a 1.4M model, but an algorithmic circuit costs far less; the deciding variable is how many times each distinct problem is seen in 7,000 steps.

### Cited Findings
- Models "can and only can store 2 bits of knowledge per parameter," robust to int8 quantization; a 7B model would hold ~14B bits. Paper submitted 8 April 2024. — [Allen-Zhu & Li, arXiv:2404.05405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05405)
- With 1,000 exposures per knowledge piece, peak capacity is ≥2 bits/param; with 100 exposures it is ≥1 bit/param ("undertraining costs no more than 2x"). — [arXiv:2404.05405 full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05405)
- Experiments use GPT-2 models from ~1M to 0.5B parameters on synthetic biographies bioS(N), N from 10K to 10M people; the 2-bit peak holds "irrespective of depth or width" for depth ≥2; 1-layer models are slightly lower, especially at 100 exposures. [≤10M through 100M–1B] — [arXiv:2404.05405 full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05405)
- Junk data: when 7/8 of training tokens are low-quality/unlearnable, useful-knowledge capacity can fall by up to 20x at 100 exposures (far more than the 8x token share would predict); prepending a domain/special token to the useful data cuts the loss factor from 20x to 2x. — [arXiv:2404.05405 full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05405)
- "1000 exposures" runs took ~140K steps (N=10K, batch 24) up to ~1M steps (N=10M), 512-token windows; 100-exposure runs ~10x shorter. The bioS knowledge per person is ~47.6 bits excluding names. — [arXiv:2404.05405 full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05405)
- Toolformer scaling experiment: the ability to use tools "emerges at around 775M parameters"; GPT-2 124M and 355M performed similarly with and without tools (exception: Wikipedia search on QA). [100M–1B, ≥1B] — [Schick et al., arXiv:2302.04761](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)

### Inferences
- Capacity budget: 1.4M params × 2 bits ≈ 2.8M bits (1000-exposure regime) or ≈1.4M bits (100-exposure regime). Rough count of distinct (a, b, op) triples with results < 256: ~33K additions (a+b ≤ 255), ~33K subtractions, ~2K multiplications, ≈68K facts; at 8 bits per result that is ≈0.54M bits. Pure memorization would therefore cost ≈270K params (19% of the model) at 2 bits/param or ≈540K params (39%) at 1 bit/param. This is an upper bound: an algorithmic circuit (see Q5) needs far fewer parameters than a lookup table, and most of those triples are never sampled in 7,000 steps.
- The exposure result is the binding constraint, not the bit count. Allen-Zhu & Li needed 100–1,000 exposures per fact; at 7,000 steps with a mixed batch, a corpus of even a few thousand distinct word problems gives each problem only a handful of exposures, which is far below the regime where "stored bits" is meaningful. In that regime the no-tool model either learns a generalizing algorithm or does not learn arithmetic at all; either way, a lookup-table capacity cost is not the right mental model.
- The 20x junk-data result shows that low-exposure, hard-to-learn data can crowd out capacity super-linearly, which is the strongest published support for the direction of the user's hypothesis. But "junk" there was unlearnable noise; arithmetic has structure, so the analogy is loose.
- Toolformer's 775M threshold is for *choosing* when to call a tool in free text at 6.7B-model-generated data; the user's tool calls are in-distribution training targets, so the threshold does not transfer directly, but it is a warning that a 1.4M model may be spending capacity learning tool-call syntax rather than freeing it.

### Gaps
- Allen-Zhu & Li measure *factual* bits; no paper gives bits-per-parameter for an *algorithmic* skill like addition, so the capacity cost of arithmetic cannot be read off a bits/param law.
- The full text beyond ~100K characters (MoE and quantization sections) was not read; no claims from it are included.
- Whether the user's 1.4M count includes embeddings matters (Kaplan excludes them, Q4). With a 10K-token vocabulary and d_model 128 the embedding alone would be ~1.3M params. Not verifiable from here.

## Key question 2: Toy models of superposition and capacity (Elhage et al. 2022; Scherlis et al. 2022): how features compete and when a new task costs old ones

### Takeaway
In the toy-model theory, features share a fixed capacity budget bounded by the embedding dimension; a feature gets a full dimension, a fraction (polysemantic), or nothing depending on its marginal loss benefit relative to a global threshold, so adding an important feature does displace less important ones. The theory predicts a cost to language only when the model is saturated and arithmetic features are more loss-important than the marginal language features.

### Cited Findings
- Scherlis et al. (submitted 4 Oct 2022, v4 25 Mar 2025) define feature capacity C_i = (W_i·W_i)² / Σ_j (W_i·W_j)², in [0,1], "the fraction of a dimension" a feature occupies; total capacity Σ C_i is bounded above by the embedding dimension D (proved in their Appendix F). — [Scherlis et al., arXiv:2210.01892](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.01892)
- Optimal allocation sets each feature where its marginal loss reduction meets a Lagrange threshold λ: C_i = 0 (ignored), 0 < C_i < 1 (polysemantic/shared), or C_i = 1 (dedicated). In their quadratic toy model with input kurtosis k > 3: ignored if (k−1)/2 · v_i < λ, fully represented if v_i > λ, partially represented in between; for k ≤ 3 features are never partial. — [arXiv:2210.01892](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.01892)
- Sparser inputs have higher kurtosis (for Bernoulli(p) sparsity, E[x⁴] = Kurt[z]/p), which produces diminishing returns to capacity and hence polysemanticity; dense inputs give all-or-nothing allocation. Phase diagram computed for 6 features in 3 neurons. — [arXiv:2210.01892](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.01892)
- Abstract: "the most important features tend to get dedicated (monosemantic) representation, less important features are represented polysemantically in proportion to their effect on the loss, and the least important features are ignored." — [arXiv:2210.01892 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01892)
- Elhage et al., "Toy Models of Superposition" (submitted 21 Sep 2022, 16 authors): models store additional sparse features in superposition, which causes polysemanticity; they demonstrate "the existence of a phase change" and a connection to uniform polytope geometry. — [arXiv:2209.10652](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10652); page sections "Superposition as a Phase Change" and "The Geometry of Superposition" — [Transformer Circuits](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html)

### Inferences
- The displacement mechanism is explicit: with total capacity saturated, raising one feature's importance moves its marginal-benefit curve up and it takes capacity from others. Whether arithmetic features displace language features in the user's model depends on (i) whether total capacity is saturated after 7,000 steps and (ii) the relative loss weight of arithmetic tokens vs story tokens. If arithmetic tokens are a small share of the loss, the theory predicts arithmetic features get *less* capacity, not that stories lose much.
- In Scherlis et al.'s equal-importance example, a feature competing against N−1 others of unit importance in D dimensions gets C = D/N at equal importance, i.e. capacity splits in proportion to count when importance is equal. If arithmetic adds a modest number of extra features to a language model that already holds many, the per-feature loss to language is small, which is consistent with the ~2% perplexity difference observed either way.

### Gaps
- The body text of "Toy Models of Superposition" could not be fetched (the page is embedded images); the importance-vs-sparsity phase diagram, the "dimensions per feature" metric and the n/m setups are not quoted here and should be taken from the paper directly.
- Neither toy paper connects capacity units to transformer parameter counts, nor models two *tasks* (as opposed to features); no toy-model paper quantifies "task A costs task B X% of loss."

## Key question 3: Multi-task interference and data-mixture tradeoffs at fixed size (Data Mixing Laws, DoReMi, RegMix, code-in-pretraining)

### Takeaway
At 70M–2.8B parameters, adding a symbolic domain (code) to text pretraining is a positive-transfer story up to a large share (NL reasoning +3.4% relative at 25% code, flat to 75%), with erosion only at 90–100% code; domain-loss interactions follow an exponential-in-proportion law whose cross-terms can be negative (helpful) or positive (conflict). Nothing in this literature is at ≤10M parameters, and no study measures arithmetic-vs-prose mixing loss directly.

### Cited Findings
- Aryabumi et al., "To Code, or Not To Code?" (20 Aug 2024), 470M and 2.8B models, 200B tokens for proportion ablations: vs text-only, code gives up to +8.2% relative NL reasoning, +4.2% world knowledge, +6.6% generative win-rate, 12x code. [100M–1B, ≥1B] — [arXiv:2408.10914](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10914)
- Proportion sweep (0/25/50/75/90/100% code, 470M): NL reasoning best at 25% code (+3.4% relative to 0%), holds up to 75%, then "rapidly erodes"; 100% code is −18.3% relative. World knowledge declines monotonically: −3.4% at 25%, ~−31% at 75%, ~−86% at 100%. Code performance rises roughly linearly; 2.8B results "largely consistent" with 470M. — [arXiv:2408.10914 full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10914)
- Cooldown (40B tokens) with 20% code: NL reasoning +3.6%, world knowledge +10.1%, code +20% vs no cooldown; without code, no NL-reasoning gain. — [arXiv:2408.10914 full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10914)
- Data Mixing Laws (Ye et al., 25 Mar 2024; ICLR 2025): validation loss on domain i is L_i = c_i + k_i·exp(Σ_j t_ij r_j) in mixture proportions r_j; two-domain pilot (GitHub vs Pile-CC) at 70M and 160M, 30B tokens, GitHub proportions {0.25,…,0.75}; log-scale domain loss was linear in proportion. Negative t_ij means domain j helps domain i; positive means conflict; the paper's only named conflict example is "training symbolic data for prose," given qualitatively without numbers. [100M–1B] — [arXiv:2403.16952](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.16952v2)
- The optimized mixture matched a 1B model trained for 48% more steps on the default mixture; nested fits used 70M/160M/305M/410M models at 30B tokens. — [arXiv:2403.16952](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16952)
- DoReMi (Xie et al., 17 May 2023; NeurIPS 2023): a 280M proxy sets domain weights for an 8B model; "improves perplexity across all domains, even when it downweights a domain"; +6.5 points average few-shot accuracy; baseline accuracy reached with 2.6x fewer steps. [≥1B] — [arXiv:2305.10429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10429)
- Mixed math/NLI fine-tuning of Flan-T5-Base (250M), Dec 2025: math-only fine-tuning raised DeepMind-Math linear-algebra accuracy 3.1% → 12.0% while MultiNLI fell 81.0% → 16.5% "within the first 1,000 training steps"; interleaving at 1:1 gave 12.0% math and 86.2% NLI; even 15:1 math:NLI kept NLI at 83.8%. [100M–1B] — [arXiv:2512.13706](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13706v1)
- Lee et al. (Jul 2023), NanoGPT 10.6M: Shakespeare text (1,115,394 characters) mixed with 10,000 plain-addition and 3,000 detailed-scratchpad examples; with enough addition examples, addition accuracy "stayed high" even with text prompts; few-shot exemplars improved it; the text explains the gain as "the advantageous impact of text prompts originates from the combined text and addition data." [≤10M] — [arXiv:2307.03381](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)

### Inferences
- Across 70M–2.8B the sign of cross-domain transfer is not reliably negative: a symbolic domain at ≤25–75% share helped or was neutral for NL reasoning. The user's assumption that arithmetic must *cost* language is not the default expectation of this literature; a small positive transfer (number words, quantity reasoning in stories) is as plausible as a small cost, which would also explain the observed 15.33 < 15.66 direction.
- Interference is dramatic when a domain is *removed* from the mixture (Flan-T5-Base: −64.5 points in <1,000 steps) and nearly absent when it is kept at any ratio ≥1:15. The user's two models both keep stories in the mix throughout, so this literature predicts small differences in story perplexity, which matches the observation.
- DoReMi's "improves all domains" result is the clearest evidence that mixture proportions trade off *efficiency* rather than fixed capacity at 8B; at 1.4M the fixed-capacity term should be larger, but no data exist.

### Gaps
- RegMix (Liu et al. 2024) was not fetched; no claims from it are included.
- No paper measures prose perplexity as a function of arithmetic-data share at ≤10M parameters. Lee et al. plotted "NanoGPT, Perplexity" in an Appendix C.3 tandem-training figure, but the values and discussion are not in the fetched text; this is the single most relevant missing number and should be read from the PDF.
- No data-mixing study varies *tool-call* data vs *computed-answer* data.

## Key question 4: Capacity-limited vs step-limited regimes (Kaplan 2020, Chinchilla 2022): would freed capacity be visible at 7,000 steps?

### Takeaway
In Kaplan's fit L(N,S) = (N_c/N)^0.077 + (S_c/S_min)^0.76, the step term decays fast (≈0.40 nats at 7,000 steps with WebText constants) while the parameter term for a ~1M non-embedding model is ≈4 nats, so a 1M-parameter model is parameter-limited, not step-limited, long before 7,000 steps; TinyStories' own size sweep confirms 1M-parameter models are far from saturating the task. Freed capacity should therefore be visible in principle, but only if arithmetic actually occupies a non-trivial share of parameters.

### Cited Findings
- Kaplan et al. (Jan 2020), Table 3: L(N, S_min) = (N_c/N)^α_N + (S_c/S_min)^α_S with N_c ≈ 6.5×10¹³, α_N ≈ 0.077, S_c ≈ 2.1×10³, α_S ≈ 0.76; N counts non-embedding parameters only ("yields cleaner scaling laws"); models ranged from 768 to 1.5B non-embedding parameters. — [Kaplan et al., arXiv:2001.08361](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.08361)
- Training curves "follow predictable power-laws whose parameters are roughly independent of the model size," but only "after an initial transient period"; the fits "break down very early in training." "Larger models are significantly more sample-efficient," reaching a given loss in fewer steps; sample efficiency improves by almost 100x from the smallest to a very large model (Fig. 19). — [arXiv:2001.08361](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.08361)
- Critical batch size B_crit(L) = B*/L^(1/α_B), B* ≈ 2×10⁸ tokens, α_B ≈ 0.21, depends only on loss; ~1–2M tokens at convergence for the largest models. — [arXiv:2001.08361](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.08361)
- Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 29 Mar 2022): over 400 models from 70M to over 16B parameters on 5B–500B tokens; model size and training tokens should scale together ("each doubling of model size calls for a doubling of training tokens"); Chinchilla 70B used 4x Gopher's data at the same compute. [100M–1B, ≥1B] — [arXiv:2203.15556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556)
- TinyStories (May 2023), single-prompt GPT-4 scores (grammar/creativity/consistency out of 10): 1M-8L 6/3/2; 2.5M-8L 5/6/3; 8.3M-8L 7/5/5; 28M-8L 9/6/9; 21M-1L 8/3/7; 33M-2L 7/6/4. The 1M 8-layer model "answers no factual prompts correctly and often produces nonsensical or ungrammatical text." [≤10M] — [Eldan & Li, arXiv:2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- Eval loss at hidden 768: 2 layers 1.38/1.34/1.33 (2/4/8 heads); 1 layer 1.58/1.56/1.54. — [arXiv:2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)

### Inferences
- Illustrative plug-in (Kaplan WebText constants, which do *not* describe TinyStories; direction only): N-term for N = 10⁶ non-embedding is (6.5×10⁷)^0.077 ≈ 4.0 nats; S-term at S_min = 7,000 is (0.3)^0.76 ≈ 0.40 nats, at 20,000 steps ≈ 0.18 nats. The parameter term dominates by ~10:1 at 7,000 steps. So "not capacity-limited at this training length" is unlikely for a ~1M model; the more likely reading of the small perplexity gap is that arithmetic consumes little of the model, or that the two data streams differ in ways other than capacity (see Q8).
- Chinchilla heuristic applied to the user: tokens = 7,000 × batch × 64. At batch 32 that is 14M tokens (~10 tokens/param); at batch 64, 29M (~20/param); at batch 128, 57M (~41/param). The user is near the Chinchilla-optimal ratio rather than grossly undertrained, but Chinchilla's fit starts at 70M parameters and the ratio at 1.4M is unmeasured.
- Because larger models are ~100x more sample-efficient (Kaplan Fig. 19), a 1.4M model is the slowest learner of any skill, including tool-call syntax; equal step counts for the two variants do not mean equal "effective training" on the shared story data.

### Gaps
- No scaling-law paper reports the L(N,S) constants for TinyStories-like data; the plug-in numbers above are a directional sanity check only.
- TinyStories' full eval-loss grid (hidden 64–1024 × layers 1–12) is a figure not available as text; only the hidden-768 row above was readable.
- No paper identifies the step count at which a ≤10M model's language loss becomes parameter-limited on TinyStories.

## Key question 5: What arithmetic costs a transformer (Nanda 2023; Quirke & Barez 2023; Lee 2023; Stolfo 2023; Zhou 2024); size needed for 2-digit add/sub/mul from word problems

### Takeaway
Known arithmetic circuits are small: one layer with 3–4 heads and a few hundred MLP neurons suffices for modular addition (p=113) and 5–15-digit addition, and a 10.6M NanoGPT learns 3-digit addition nearly perfectly from ~2,500 reversed-format examples (plain format plateaus at ~85% at 10,000 examples). Multiplication is much harder at 10.6M (≈5–20% accuracy on 2-digit operands with 3,000 examples). No paper trains arithmetic from *word problems* at ≤10M parameters, so the parameter cost of parsing the story around the numbers is unmeasured.

### Cited Findings
- Nanda et al. (Jan 2023): 1-layer ReLU transformer, d_model 128, 4 heads of d_head 32, d_mlp 512, modulus 113, 30% of 113² pairs for training, full-batch AdamW lr 1e-3, weight decay 1, 40,000 epochs; test accuracy near 100% after ~10,000 epochs; phases: memorization 0–1.4k, circuit formation 1.4k–9.4k, cleanup 9.4k–14k. 5 key frequencies used; 433 of 512 MLP neurons (84.6%) have >85% of variance explained by a single frequency. [≤10M] — [arXiv:2301.05217](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.05217)
- Quirke & Barez (ICLR 2024, arXiv 2310.13121): one-layer transformer, 3 heads (2 or 4 also work), 5-digit addition (also 10 and 15 digits), 5,000 epochs × batch 64 (~1.5–1.8M examples out of 10¹⁰ possible), final loss 0.009; per-digit parallel computation with distinct algorithms for first/middle/last digits; "Base Add," "Make Carry," "Use Carry" sub-tasks mapped to specific heads; rare carry cascades ("Use Sum 9") only partially implemented, attributed to the model's small size. [≤10M] — [arXiv:2310.13121](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13121v8)
- Lee et al. (Jul 2023), NanoGPT 6 layers, 6 heads, d=384, ~10.6M params, character-level tokens: 3-digit addition in reversed format shows a phase transition between ~1,000 and ~4,000 samples (≈2,500) to near-perfect accuracy; plain format plateaus "slightly over 85%" even at 10,000 samples; detailed scratchpad reaches perfect addition with ~1,000 samples; GPT-2 (12L, 12H, 768) outperformed NanoGPT on both plain and scratchpad addition. [≤10M, 100M–1B] — [arXiv:2307.03381](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- Lee et al. multiplication: joint training with 3,000 examples covering all 100 one-digit×one-digit cases plus random ≤2-digit operands; Table 4 accuracies range ~4.6% to 19.6% across conditions; detailed scratchpad helps, reversal "not particularly effective." Subtraction: only qualitative, scratchpad variant that handles negatives in post-processing is better. — [arXiv:2307.03381](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- Lee et al. GPT-3 fine-tuning (1,000 samples, 4 epochs, Davinci): addition pretrained 2% → plain 34%, reverse 80.9%, detailed scratchpad 99.5%; subtraction pretrained 0.1% → plain 84.8%, detailed scratchpad 99.5%. [≥1B] — [arXiv:2307.03381](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- Stolfo et al. (EMNLP 2023): causal mediation on 2.8B, 6B, 7B models shows arithmetic is carried by a small set of mid-late layers; attention moves operand information to the final token and MLPs there produce result information. [≥1B] — [arXiv:2305.15054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15054)
- Zhou et al. (Feb 2024): with the right format and position encoding, standard transformers extrapolate addition to 2.5x the trained length, but generalization is fragile and "strongly affected by random weight initialization and training data order" (large variance across seeds). — [arXiv:2402.09371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09371)
- Toolformer (Feb 2023) math word problems, 6.7B: GPT-J 7.5/5.2/9.9 on ASDiv/SVAMP/MAWPS; Toolformer with API calls disabled 14.8/6.3/15.0; with calculator 40.4/29.4/44.0; GPT-3 175B 14.0/10.0/19.8. [≥1B] — [arXiv:2302.04761](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.04761)

### Inferences
- Parameter cost of an addition circuit: Nanda's config implies roughly 2×10⁵ parameters total (embedding 113×128 ≈ 14.5K, attention 4×(3×128×32)+128² ≈ 65K, MLP 2×128×512 ≈ 131K, unembed ≈ 14.5K; my arithmetic from the stated config, the paper gives no count), of which the MLP's 433 frequency-specific neurons are the bulk. A dedicated 1-layer circuit for byte-range addition is therefore on the order of 10% of a 1.4M model if built from scratch, but inside a language model the circuit shares the embedding, attention and residual stream, so the *marginal* cost is mostly MLP neurons, plausibly a few hundred.
- Format matters more than size in the 10M regime: plain-format (most-significant-digit-first) addition never exceeds ~85% at 10.6M params. The user's no-tool model writes answers in plain decimal order inside prose, which is the hardest format; if it has not reached high arithmetic accuracy, it may not have built a circuit at all, in which case there is no capacity to free.
- Multiplication at 2-digit operands was the weak spot even at 10.6M with 3,000 examples; results under 256 restrict operands (e.g. ≤15×16), which is a much smaller table (≈2K facts) and probably memorizable.
- Zhou et al.'s seed sensitivity, combined with a ~2% perplexity gap, means a single-seed comparison cannot distinguish capacity effects from init noise; at least 3–5 seeds per variant are needed.

### Gaps
- No published result trains add/sub/mul from *word problems* (numbers embedded in sentences, answer in words) at ≤10M parameters; the minimum size for that joint parse-and-compute task is unknown.
- Quirke & Barez do not state d_model, d_mlp or a parameter count; no paper reports the arithmetic circuit's parameter cost as a fraction of a model that also does language.
- Lee et al.'s per-condition multiplication table could not be mapped to conditions from the fetched text.

## Key question 6: "Use it or lose it": decay of skills absent from continued training, and of internal arithmetic after tool training

### Takeaway
Skills absent from the training stream decay fast at small scale (Lee et al.: plain-format 1–3-digit addition is "forgotten entirely" when a 10.6M model is fine-tuned on 4-digit data; Flan-T5-Base: NLI 81% → 16.5% within 1,000 math-only steps) and more mildly at billion scale (4–8% of math items forgotten after instruction tuning, offset by 16–19% backward transfer). Tool training specifically has *not* been shown to erase internal arithmetic: Toolformer with calls disabled still beat its base model on math; newer work (2025–26, ≥1B, mostly frontier models) finds tool use shifts behavior away from deliberative reasoning rather than deleting skills. In the user's design the tool model never acquires computation in the first place, which is a different question from decay.

### Cited Findings
- Lee et al. (10.6M NanoGPT), Section 7.2: fine-tuning a 3-digit-addition model on 4-digit data, the plain format "forgets" 1–3-digit additions entirely; reverse and scratchpad formats retain some; detailed scratchpad keeps 1–3-digit accuracy high with a brief dip that recovers. Fine-tuning needs ~1,000–5,000 examples of the new length for reverse/scratchpad. [≤10M] — [arXiv:2307.03381](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- Flan-T5-Base (250M): math-only fine-tuning drops MultiNLI 81.0% → 16.5% "within the first 1,000 training steps"; 1:1 interleaving keeps NLI at 86.2%. [100M–1B] — [arXiv:2512.13706](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13706v1)
- Luo et al. (Aug 2023, v5 Jan 2025): 5 sequential instruction tasks, 100K samples each, 3 epochs; BLOOMZ domain-knowledge (MMLU) drops 3.35 pts (1.1B) → 7.47 (7.1B); reasoning drops 5.40 → 9.91; reading comprehension (RACE) 9.49 → 15.74; relative forgetting rises with scale ("increasingly severe as the model scale increases"); mT0-3.7B forgets more than BLOOMZ-3B (20.15 vs 14.63 FG on MMLU). The reasoning set includes MathQA. [≥1B] — [arXiv:2308.08747](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.08747)
- Harmon et al. (20 Oct 2025): chance-adjusted forgetting after instruction tuning, Qwen2.5-Instruct 3B/7B/14B/32B: Math 7.7/4.2/6.2/4.5%, Logic 10.9/8.4/5.5/4.6%, Culture 11.7/16.5/14.0/15.2%, Knowledge 6.8/15.0/23.4/15.3%; Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct Math 7.3%; math backward transfer ~16–19%; total forgetting falls 8.5% (3B) → 6.7% (32B); Qwen2.5-Math-7B (math CPT) shows 12.9% total forgetting vs 6.4–8.0% for Coder CPT. [≥1B] — [arXiv:2510.17776](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.17776v1)
- Toolformer (6.7B GPT-J base): with API calls disabled, math scores 14.8/6.3/15.0 (ASDiv/SVAMP/MAWPS) vs base GPT-J 7.5/5.2/9.9, i.e. internal arithmetic *improved* after tool fine-tuning; language-model perplexity WikiText 10.3 / CCNet 10.5 for Toolformer-disabled vs 9.9 / 10.6 for GPT-J, identical to the "GPT-J + CC" control (10.3 / 10.5), so the perplexity change comes from the fine-tuning corpus, not the tool. Perplexity with tools enabled not reported. [≥1B] — [arXiv:2302.04761](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.04761)
- Calc-X / Calcformers (Kadlčík et al., May 2023): T5/FLAN-T5 ~700M and 3B fine-tuned to emit sympy calculator calls; GSM8K 39.6 vs 19.2 (T5-XL), Ape210K 53.8 vs 20.8, ASDiv-A 66.8 vs 37.0; "2–3 times" more accurate than self-computing baselines; no language-modeling or non-math metrics reported; own arithmetic without the calculator not measured. [100M–1B, ≥1B] — [arXiv:2305.15017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.15017)
- "Selective Forgetting in Tool-Augmented Language-Model Agents" (NeurIPS 2026 listing; Wu, Nourzad, Roknilamouki, Ekici, Liang, Shroff): tool-use fine-tuning of Qwen-based models "does not broadly degrade" math/commonsense/instruction benchmarks but shifts the model "away from deliberative reasoning": a growing Tool-On vs Tool-Off gap, reduced reflection-marker density, "substantial response-length compression"; no numbers in the listing. [≥1B] — [NeurIPS 2026 listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/165633)
- Tool-Induced Myopia (Fatahi Bayat et al., 14 Nov 2025, ACL 2026): seven proprietary models with/without a code interpreter on 1,000 competition-math problems; tools raise final accuracy (avg 37.6% → 43.3%; o4-mini 45.1 → 64.4) but base models win pairwise reasoning comparisons more often (avg win rate 52.4% vs 47.6%; GPT-4.1-mini 58.6 vs 41.4); tool use "reduces arithmetic errors but increases logic, assumption, and creativity errors." Effect is from off-the-shelf tool access, not fine-tuning; removal-after-training not tested. [≥1B, proprietary] — [arXiv:2511.10899](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.10899v1)
- Pre-Calc (Apr 2024): BERT/RoBERTa/Flan-T5 pre-finetuned to use a calculator; improves numeracy tasks; no general-language metrics reported on the abstract page. [100M–1B] — [arXiv:2404.14355](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14355)

### Inferences
- Decay is real, fast, and scale-dependent in the wrong direction for small models: the two smallest-scale results (10.6M, 250M) show near-total loss of an unexercised skill within ~1,000 steps of training on something else, whereas 3B–32B models lose single-digit percentages. If the user ever switches the tool model to a no-tool regime (or vice versa) mid-training, expect the switched-off skill to vanish within a fraction of the 7,000-step budget unless the old data is kept in the mix (Lee's "include all shorter lengths," Flan-T5's 1:1 interleave).
- For the tool model as designed, "use it or lose it" is moot: the skill of computing was never trained, so there is nothing to decay. The relevant question becomes whether tool-call syntax is cheaper to learn than computing; at 1.4M params with context 64, learning a reliable call-and-copy pattern is itself a non-trivial induction/copy circuit.
- Toolformer is the only published case that measures both tool-off arithmetic and perplexity after tool training, and it shows no loss of internal arithmetic and no perplexity gain attributable to the tool. That is a null result for "freed capacity" at 6.7B, where capacity is not binding.
- The 2025–26 agent papers suggest the cost of tool reliance is behavioral (shorter, less reflective reasoning) rather than parametric; a 1.4M story model has no comparable reasoning policy, so this literature is of limited relevance.

### Gaps
- No study measures decay of *internal arithmetic* in a model trained from scratch with calculator calls, at any scale; Calc-X and Pre-Calc did not test tool-off arithmetic or non-math performance.
- The "Selective Forgetting" paper's numbers (sizes, gap magnitudes) are not in the listing; the full paper was not available.
- No paper at ≤10M parameters measures forgetting of arithmetic during continued *language-only* pretraining (the exact "lose it" condition for the user's no-tool model if arithmetic data were removed).

## Key question 7: TinyStories (Eldan & Li 2023): what 1–30M models can and cannot do, and reported tradeoffs

### Takeaway
At ~1M parameters a TinyStories model gets grammar ~6/10 but consistency ~2/10 and answers no factual prompts; consistency emerges when hidden size goes from 64 to 128, and depth matters more for consistency/context tracking while width matters more for facts. The paper reports no ability-vs-ability tradeoffs at fixed size, only that different abilities saturate at different sizes and training stages.

### Cited Findings
- Dataset: synthetic stories with a ~1,500-word vocabulary (3–4-year-old level), generated by GPT-3.5/GPT-4; HF card lists ~2.12M training and ~22K validation stories (2,141,709 rows, 7.62 GB), with models TinyStories-1M/3M/8M/28M/33M and a 1-layer 21M variant. — [Eldan & Li, arXiv:2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759); [HF dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/roneneldan/TinyStories)
- Architecture/training: GPT-Neo with window 256 and context 512, GPT-Neo tokenizer restricted to the top 10K tokens; trainable on one V100 in ≤~30 hours. [≤10M] — [arXiv:2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- Size dependence: "Consistency emerges when hidden size rises from 64 to 128"; shallower models do better on grammar than consistency; grammar plateaus earlier in training than consistency or creativity; factual knowledge depends more on embedding dimension, context tracking more on layer count; one-layer models struggle with instruction following, two layers suffice for some. — [arXiv:2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- Scores (one prompt, grammar/creativity/consistency): 1M-8L 6/3/2; 2.5M-8L 5/6/3; 8.3M-8L 7/5/5; 28M-8L 9/6/9; 21M-1L 8/3/7; 33M-2L 7/6/4. Largest (~80M) model near-perfect grammar and consistency but well below GPT-4 on creativity. — [arXiv:2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- Interpretability: in a 1-layer 16-head model, some heads are positional (fixed relative distance) and some semantic (attending to "banana," "park," or the protagonists); single neurons for subject pronouns, action words, adjectives, and first appearance of the protagonist. — [arXiv:2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- Baseline: ~125M GPT-2/GPT-Neo trained on ordinary corpora "rarely produce coherent text beyond a few words." — [arXiv:2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759)

### Inferences
- The user's 1.4M model sits at the bottom of TinyStories' sweep (TinyStories-1M), where consistency and facts are essentially absent and grammar is mediocre; in Scherlis-style terms, many language features are already being dropped or shared, so capacity is saturated on stories alone. Any arithmetic circuit must displace something, but the displaced features may be low-importance ones whose loss contribution is small, consistent with a ~2% perplexity effect in either direction.
- Perplexity 15–16 on TinyStories-style text is high relative to the 1.33–1.58 eval *loss* (nats, ≈3.8–4.9 perplexity) of the hidden-768 models, which is consistent with a heavily parameter-limited model and a 64-token context (TinyStories used 512).

### Gaps
- The paper's Figure 4 (averaged GPT-4 scores per size) and the full eval-loss grid are images, not text; only the single-prompt scores above were extractable.
- TinyStories reports no experiment that adds a second skill at fixed size, so it gives no direct capacity-tradeoff number.

## Key question 8: Direct answers for the 1.4M-parameter setting: (a) does arithmetic plausibly consume a measurable share, (b) when would freed capacity show up, (c) does the offloaded skill decay?

### Takeaway
(a) Plausibly yes but small: a dedicated byte-range addition circuit is on the order of a few hundred MLP neurons (Nanda: 433 of 512 neurons at d_model 128), i.e. single-digit percent of 1.4M params; a memorization-style solution would cost 20–40% but requires 100–1,000 exposures per fact, which 7,000 steps cannot provide. (b) Freed capacity would show up only if the no-tool model actually learned to compute (check its arithmetic accuracy; plain-format accuracy at 10.6M plateaus at ~85%), with ≥3–5 seeds (Zhou et al. seed variance), and in a parameter-limited regime, which Kaplan's fit and TinyStories' size sweep say a 1M model is in at 7,000 steps. (c) Nothing is offloaded-then-forgotten in the user's design; the tool model never learns to compute. Decay would only apply if the no-tool model were later trained without arithmetic data, in which case small-model evidence predicts near-total loss within ~1,000 steps.

### Cited Findings
- 2 bits/param at 1,000 exposures, 1 bit/param at 100 exposures, tested on ~1M–0.5B GPT-2 models. — [arXiv:2404.05405](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05405)
- Nanda's modular-addition circuit: 1 layer, d_model 128, 4 heads, 512 MLP neurons, 433 frequency-specific. — [arXiv:2301.05217](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.05217)
- 10.6M NanoGPT: plain-format 3-digit addition plateaus ~85% at 10,000 examples; reversed reaches ~100% after ~2,500 examples; plain-format 1–3-digit addition forgotten entirely when trained only on 4-digit. — [arXiv:2307.03381](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- Flan-T5-Base NLI 81.0% → 16.5% within 1,000 math-only steps; 1:1 mix prevents it. — [arXiv:2512.13706](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13706v1)
- Length generalization of addition "strongly affected by random weight initialization and training data order." — [arXiv:2402.09371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09371)
- Kaplan L(N,S) constants: α_N ≈ 0.077, α_S ≈ 0.76, S_c ≈ 2.1×10³. — [arXiv:2001.08361](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.08361)
- 25% code improved NL reasoning by 3.4% relative at 470M; symbolic data is not automatically a cost to prose. — [arXiv:2408.10914](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10914)
- Toolformer-disabled perplexity equal to the non-tool fine-tuning control (10.3 vs 10.3 WikiText). — [arXiv:2302.04761](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.04761)

### Inferences
- Confounds that could produce the observed 15.66 (tool) > 15.33 (no tool) without any capacity story: (i) the calculator-call string occupies tokens inside a 64-token window, so each arithmetic sample in the tool stream displaces story tokens and shortens the effective story context; (ii) the two streams have different token statistics (digits appear as copied tool output vs generated), so "story perplexity" may be measured on slightly different conditioning; (iii) single-seed noise of order the observed gap. None of these is addressed by the literature; all are cheap to test (equalize story-token counts per step, hold out a pure-story validation set with identical tokenization, run 3–5 seeds).
- A cleaner test of the hypothesis at this scale, suggested by the literature's own designs: train a third variant with *no arithmetic at all* at matched story-token count (the Aryabumi 0%-code control), and measure both story perplexity and arithmetic accuracy for all three. If no-arithmetic ≈ no-tool ≈ tool on story perplexity, arithmetic costs nothing measurable at 1.4M/7,000 steps; if no-arithmetic is best, the cost exists and the tool variant should sit between.
- Freed capacity is more likely to appear as a *story-perplexity* difference at longer training (the S-term is already small by 7,000 steps, so differences are parameter-driven, but the arithmetic circuit itself forms late: Nanda's circuit formed over epochs 1.4k–9.4k and Lee's phase transition needs thousands of examples). Checking the no-tool model's arithmetic accuracy over training would tell whether a circuit exists yet.

### Gaps
- The literature has no controlled experiment at any scale that holds parameters fixed and compares held-out language perplexity with vs without calculator offloading; the user's experiment would be the first such measurement I could find.
- No measurement of how many parameters an arithmetic circuit occupies *inside* a language model (as opposed to a standalone arithmetic model).
- No small-model (≤10M) evidence on whether learning tool-call syntax is cheaper than learning the computation; Toolformer's 775M threshold is for a different (free-text, self-supervised) setting.
