# Measuring "language and other skills" in tiny story-trained LMs (0.5–10 M params), and what mixing math/code/structured data does to them

Scope note: the user's setting is a 1.4 M-weight GPT, ~4,000-word vocabulary, 64-token context, ~7,000 CPU steps, TinyStories-style stories mixed with small arithmetic word problems; one variant delegates arithmetic to an external calculator via an in-text call, the other computes answers itself. All measures below are chosen to be runnable on a laptop CPU without GPT-4 grading at scale. Dates and model sizes are given wherever the source gives them. Where the literature is silent, this is stated explicitly in Gaps.

## KQ1. How TinyStories evaluated grammar, creativity, consistency and plot; cheaper proxies; what 1–3 M models scored; which abilities appear first with width vs depth

### Takeaway
TinyStories (Eldan & Li, May 2023) graded ~50 held-out story prompts × 10 temperature-1 completions per model with GPT-4 on a 1–10 scale for grammar, creativity, consistency (and plot/instruction-consistency for the Instruct variant); the paper's own text says grammar is learned by very small/shallow models and plateaus first, consistency and context-tracking need depth and hidden size ≥128, factual recall tracks embedding dimension, and instruction following needs ≥2 layers. The per-size score table is only an image, but a text table at hidden size 768 shows eval loss and GPT-4 scores moving together (a 0.04–0.05 nat loss drop ≈ +0.3–0.6 grading points).

### Cited Findings
- GPT-Eval procedure: grader sees a manually written story beginning plus the model's completion, gives a verbal assessment then numeric grades out of 10; the instruction is to "grade the student's completion in terms of grammar, creativity, consistency" (plus whether the plot makes sense) and to guess the writer's age band ("A: 3 or under. B: 4-5. C: 6-7. D: 8-9. E: 10-12. F: 13-16."). About 50 manually prepared prompts, 10 completions each at temperature 1, scores averaged. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2); [TinyStories abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759)
- A follow-up paper reuses the prompt "with small modifications" and the output format "Grammar: 8, Creativity: 7, Consistency:5, Plot: 6" (1–10 scale), confirming a four-dimension variant including plot. — [Auto-Regressive Next-Token Predictors are Universal Learners](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.06979)
- TinyStories-Instruct: each story is preceded by a random subset of four instruction types (word list, a required sentence, features such as dialogue/bad ending/plot twist, a 1–2 line summary); evaluated on ~50 instruction sets disjoint from training with GPT-4 scoring instruction-consistency and plot; an OOD variant withholds the summary+word-list combination and a 33 M model still follows both. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Width vs depth statements from the paper text: shallower models do relatively better on grammar than on consistency; grammar plateaus earlier than consistency or creativity; consistency with the story's beginning appears when hidden size grows from 64 to 128; one-layer models struggle with instruction following, two layers suffice for some; instruction following depends more on depth, plot coherence more on hidden dimension; factual knowledge depends more on embedding dimension, context tracking more on layer count; a 1-layer model got no consistency prompts right but some facts right, a 64-dimension model got no facts right but kept consistency in several cases; "even very small models (2.5M) or single-layer models" show some factual knowledge. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Manual probe prompts are grouped into factual, reasoning and context-tracking categories (Figures 9–11); e.g. "Alice was so tired when she got back home so she went" → "to bed"/"straight to bed" for small TinyStories models vs "outside" for GPT-2-XL. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Loss vs grades: the paper reports no perplexity but states GPT-Eval scores are "consistently increasing with the decrease of evaluation loss" (shown across training for GPT-Neo models), with the caveat that some capabilities emerge at different sizes. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Only text-extractable score table (hidden size 768; columns layers / heads / eval loss / grammar / creativity / consistency): 2L-2H 1.38 / 7.77 / 6.5 / 7.78; 2L-4H 1.34 / 8.05 / 6.57 / 8.16; 2L-8H 1.33 / 8.25 / 6.53 / 8.16; 1L-2H 1.58 / 7.13 / 5.83 / 6.38; 1L-4H 1.56 / 7.43 / 5.90 / 6.75; 1L-8H 1.54 / 7.45 / 6.28 / 7.02. More heads help all metrics when head count is small; creativity does not move in the 2-layer rows. — [TinyStories HTML, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Scaling section varied layers over {2, 4, 8, 12} and hidden dims from 64 to 2048; largest TinyStories model ≈80 M params reaches near-perfect grammar and consistency but falls well short of GPT-4 on creativity. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Tokenizer/vocab: GPT-Neo tokenizer truncated to the top 10 K tokens; GPT-Neo architecture, window 256, context 512. — [TinyStories HTML, footnote 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Interpretability at 1 layer × 1024 hidden × 16 heads: distance-based heads (fixed relative offsets) and semantic heads (e.g. "the"/"a" → "banana", most words → names "Tom"/"Lucy"); in the 1 M model, neurons for subject pronouns, actions, adjectives, and one that fires on first introduction of the protagonist. — [TinyStories HTML, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Cheap-judge precedent: SimpleStories (NeurIPS 2025 D&B) trains 1.25 M–35 M models with a 4,096-token WordPiece tokenizer and scores grammar / coherence / quality / originality on a 0–100 scale with a GPT-based judge (GPT-4o-mini is the judge named elsewhere in the paper) on N = 200 generated stories per model with 99.9 % CIs; all SimpleStories sizes beat TinyStories-33M on all four metrics; the 1.25 M model's probes reach only ~50 % relative accuracy; a Llama-architecture TinyStories-33M still trails SimpleStories-35M. — [SimpleStories paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09184); the 33 M model card reports Grammar 90.8, Coherence 85.7, Quality 81.5, Originality 72.5 — [SimpleStories-35M README](https://huggingface.co/SimpleStories/SimpleStories-35M/blob/main/README.md)
- SimpleStories also uses judge-free text statistics as proxies: compression ratio, Self-BLEU, n-gram diversity, POS-template rate (88.9 SimpleStories vs 100 TinyStories), templates per token (0.016 vs 0.026), top-4-gram share ("once upon a time" in 59.38 % of TinyStories samples vs 7.49 % for its top 4-gram), Flesch–Kincaid grade (3.08 vs 2.55). — [SimpleStories paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09184)
- TinyEvals (AI Safety Camp 2024, lead Jett Janiak) characterises TinyStories 1–33 M models bottom-up: collect correct-and-confident next-token predictions on the validation set, find the simplest common pattern, define it as (prompt, correct_answer) pairs, and score each model by probability of the correct answer, rank of the correct answer, and logit difference vs an obvious wrong answer. Capabilities already listed for the 1 M model: n-grams, repeated tokens, repeated multi-token names (possibly induction), common phrases, plural↔singular, recognising a story should start after a context, closing quotes, pronoun resolution, related concepts ("zebra" → "stripes"), indirect-object identification. — [TinyEvals post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/aHE6rejJEkWspwnKu/aisc-project-tinyevals)
- The delphi toolkit trains small "stories-*" models with a reversible GPT-2-style BPE tokenizer of 4,096 tokens (`delphi-suite/stories-tokenizer`), 512-token sequences, supporting any HF CausalLM. — [delphi repo](https://github.com/delphi-suite/delphi)

### Inferences
- For a 1.4 M model (likely hidden ≤128, a few layers), the paper's own trajectory predicts: grammar well above chance and already near plateau, consistency marginal (it switches on around hidden 128), creativity low and flat, facts/instruction following weak. Hence "room for language skills" should be looked for in consistency/context-tracking and in grammar fine-detail, not in creativity, which barely moves at this size.
- The 768-hidden table gives a usable conversion for the user's loss comparisons: differences of ~0.04 nats in held-out loss corresponded to ~0.3–0.6 GPT-4 grading points, so a tool-use vs self-compute loss gap of ≤0.01 nats on story text is unlikely to show up in any coarse grading.
- The cheapest faithful substitute for GPT-4 grading is the SimpleStories recipe (GPT-4o-mini or a local judge, N = 200 stories, four 0–100 dimensions, bootstrap CIs); the judge-free SimpleStories statistics (Self-BLEU, n-gram diversity, template rate, top-4-gram share) are fully CPU-local proxies for "creativity/diversity" and "formulaicness".
- TinyEvals' logit-difference scoring on (prompt, answer) pairs is the right general pattern for all the probes in KQ3: no generation, deterministic, one forward pass per item.

### Gaps
- The full TinyStories score table (hidden 64…1024 × layers 1…12 with grammar/creativity/consistency) is Figure 4, an image; the HTML text and the alignmentforum summary do not transcribe it, so absolute 1 M / 3 M / 8 M scores could not be verified.
- No source validates a cheaper judge against GPT-4 grades on TinyStories outputs (search found none); the TinyEvals project page gives no results, code link, or paper.

## KQ2. Grammaticality minimal pairs: BLiMP, BabyLM use at 10 M words, building a BLiMP-style test from a 4,000-word story vocabulary, typical tiny-model scores

### Takeaway
BLiMP (67 paradigms × 1,000 template-generated pairs from a >3,000-item feature-annotated vocabulary, scored by full-sentence log-probability) is the standard; at 10 M training words, causal baselines score ~0.63–0.70 and the best BabyLM systems 0.80–0.81, while agreement/morphology paradigms are the ones models learn earliest, which is what a 4,000-word story vocabulary can support. Known artifacts (ties on permutation pairs, frequency confounds) must be designed out.

### Cited Findings
- BLiMP: 67 paradigms, 1,000 pairs each, 12 phenomena with paradigm counts anaphor agreement 2, argument structure 9, binding 7, control/raising 5, determiner-noun agreement 8, ellipsis 2, filler-gap 7, irregular forms 2, island effects 8, NPI licensing 7, quantifiers 4, subject-verb agreement 6; sentences generated from linguist-crafted templates over a vocabulary of "over 3,000 items" annotated with morphological, syntactic and semantic features; a model is correct when it assigns higher probability to the acceptable sentence; aggregate human agreement 96.4 %, individual human agreement 88.6 %; GPT-2 best, ~8 points below humans overall; models strong on morphological agreement, ellipsis, control/raising, weak on argument structure, NPI licensing, quantifiers, near chance on islands; with varying LSTM/Transformer-XL training data, performance improves steadily but phenomena are learned at different rates. — [BLiMP (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.00582); [BLiMP abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.00582)
- BabyLM 2023 (strict-small = 10 M words): best baseline OPT-125M BLiMP 0.63; top submissions ELC-BERT 0.80, MLSM 0.79, McGill-BERT 0.75; strict (100 M): BootBERT 0.86, ELC-BERT 0.85, best baseline 0.75; strict models did not greatly outperform strict-small despite 10× data; curriculum learning mostly gave no consistent gains; distillation, LTG-BERT backbone, many epochs and data augmentation helped. — [Findings of BabyLM 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08165)
- BabyLM 2024 strict-small (10 M): BabyLlama baseline BLiMP 69.8, BLiMP-Supplement 59.5, (Super)GLUE 63.3, EWoK 50.7; winner GPT-BERT BLiMP 81.2, EWoK 54.6 (1:7 causal:masked objective mix); knowledge distillation and combined objectives helped; curriculum not effective on average (β = −3.6, p = 0.055); dataset creation had a positive effect on average text score (β = 4.8, p < 0.05); most models near chance on EWoK (max 58.4). — [Findings of BabyLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05149)
- A 2025/26 retrospective reports GPT-BERT 0.81 on BLiMP at 10 M words vs 0.86 at 100 M, ELC-BERT 0.80 and MiniBERTa 0.73 at 10 M, and that the larger corpus mainly helps where the smaller one already does well. — [Retrospective analysis on LLMs and infant syntax](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26539)
- Artifact warning: a simple word-frequency baseline scored unexpectedly high because "a system that returns identical logits for both sentences in a minimal pair can achieve maximal accuracy"; 22 BLiMP subtasks pair sentences that are permutations of the same words, so tie-scoring earns credit; the problem persists in a different form in the 2025 pipeline. Tokenization sensitivity is high and strict-small sometimes beats strict. — [A Comparison of Elementary Baselines for BabyLM (2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.babylm-main.16/)
- Frequency confound: in a French BabyLM, translated supplement items often pair a grammatical sentence containing a rarer content word with an ungrammatical one using a more frequent word, letting models score below chance by preferring frequency. — [French-only BabyLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17435)
- LongTail-Swap builds corpus-specific acceptable/unacceptable pairs that isolate rare-word usage, scores by average log-probability over each pair, and finds performance poor on rare words with architecture differences larger in the tail; it argues BabyLM metrics concentrate on the head of the word distribution. — [LongTail-Swap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04268)
- One 2025 analysis reports BLiMP standard errors "reaching 0.2" (units as stated in the search summary; treat with caution). — [LongTail-Swap search summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.04268)
- Small-model data point at 18 M–44 M params on 10 M words: GPT2-18M on BabyLM mix scores BLiMP-filtered 63.7 / Supplement 55.9; GPT2-44M (hidden 768, 2 layers, 8 heads) 65.6 / 58.2; Llama-20M 62.8 / 56.6 (details in KQ6). — [What Should Baby Models Read?](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06672)
- Mini Minds (BabyLM 2023): a 4-layer/8-head encoder and 6-layer/12-head decoder trained on 10 M words perform about as well as baselines twice their size (abstract gives no parameter counts or BLiMP numbers). — [Mini Minds abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03216)

### Inferences
- Feasibility: BLiMP's generation vocabulary (>3,000 items) is the same order as the user's 4,000-word story vocabulary, so template-generated minimal pairs for subject-verb agreement, determiner-noun agreement (this/these, a/an + plural), anaphor agreement (himself/herself/themselves), irregular past forms, and pronoun case are directly buildable from the story lexicon; these are exactly the categories models learn earliest, so a 1–3 M model should be above chance on them while islands/NPIs/quantifiers would be at chance and uninformative.
- Expected range: given 10 M-word causal baselines at ~0.63–0.70 overall and morphology/agreement being the easiest categories, a 1–10 M story model trained on far fewer words should land roughly 0.6–0.85 on in-vocabulary agreement paradigms; nothing in the literature pins this down for sub-10 M models, so treat it as a prior to be measured.
- Design rules from the artifacts: (1) score with strict inequality and count ties as wrong (or 0.5); (2) never use permutation pairs; (3) keep both sentences' lexical items identical except the contrasted token and ensure both variants of the contrasted token occur in training (otherwise frequency, not grammar, decides); (4) generate from the model's own training-corpus lexicon (LongTail-Swap's "corpus-specific" principle) and stratify items by training frequency of the contrasted word; (5) with 1,000 pairs per paradigm the binomial SE at p = 0.75 is ~1.4 pp, at 200 pairs ~3 pp, so use ≥500 pairs per paradigm and several training seeds if the expected tool-use effect is a few points.
- Because the BabyLM pipeline is an lm-eval fork scoring sum log-prob of each sentence (KQ7), a custom 4,000-word tokenizer only needs the pairs as a JSONL file and a causal `backend`; the full-sentence log-prob loop is ~10 lines of PyTorch for a model with a 64-token context (pairs must fit in 64 tokens, which story-vocabulary sentences do).

### Gaps
- No published BLiMP (or BLiMP-style) number exists for a model under ~18 M parameters or trained on less than 10 M words; the TinyStories paper does not run minimal pairs at all.
- The exact per-phenomenon accuracies for 5-gram/LSTM/Transformer-XL/GPT-2 (BLiMP Table 3) could not be extracted (PDF only); only the qualitative ordering (agreement easiest, islands at chance) is verified.
- The 2025 elementary-baselines paper's actual word-frequency BLiMP score and the list of the 22 affected subtasks were not extractable from the abstract page.

## KQ3. Consistency and world-tracking tests in stories (name recall, pronoun gender, object permanence, counting, who-has-what); published probes for small story models

### Takeaway
Published entity-tracking probes exist in log-probability form (correct vs incorrect continuation) and show above-chance tracking already at Pythia-70M (53.5 %) rising with log-parameters to 89.6 % at 12 B, with human-level claimed at 410 M; TinyStories itself used hand-written context-tracking prompts graded by eye, and TinyEvals found pronoun resolution and repeated-name copying in the 1 M model. No standardised probe suite exists for sub-10 M story models, so one must be built, but the scoring recipe (logit difference between the right and wrong name/pronoun/object) is well established.

### Cited Findings
- TinyStories context-tracking prompts: the paper's manual probes are split into factual, reasoning and context-tracking categories (Figures 9–11); context tracking depends more on layer count, factual knowledge more on embedding dimension; a 1-layer model got no consistency prompts right. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- TinyEvals lists "pronoun resolution", "repeated multi-token names (possibly induction)", "indirect object identification" and "predicting related concepts" among capabilities already present in the 1 M TinyStories model, scored by correct-answer probability, rank, and logit difference vs an obvious wrong answer. — [TinyEvals post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/aHE6rejJEkWspwnKu/aisc-project-tinyevals)
- Entity tracking in sub-billion models (Aug 2026 preprint): short narratives about a character tidying a room, objects moved between locations, question asks where a focus object ends up; five complexity levels (objects/locations/moves) and focus-object position F0–F3 to control for recency; implicit format scores summed log-probability of a correct vs incorrect continuation; explicit format generates a one-word location. Pythia implicit accuracy 53.5 % at 70 M rising to 89.6 % at 12 B, improving with log(parameters); base models at or below chance on the explicit (generation) format; human implicit accuracy 79.3 % (89.0 % at C1 → 76.0 % at C4); pseudoword (Wuggy) and improbable-object templates change accuracy by ≤3 pp even though the narratives are 10^8–10^27× less likely, so tracking operates over discourse structure not lexical familiarity. — [Entity tracking sub-billion HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18083); human-level "already present at 410 million parameters" — [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18083)
- Kim & Schuster (ACL 2023): entity tracking = predict an entity's final state from an initial description plus state-changing operations; among Flan-T5/GPT-3/GPT-3.5 only the code-pretrained GPT-3.5 models showed it zero-shot, but smaller T5 models fine-tuned on the task generalised to new entities and longer operation sequences; the authors conclude text-only pretraining does not make the capacity surface. — [Entity Tracking in Language Models](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.213)
- A widely cited earlier probing claim that entity states are linearly decodable was later shown to derive mostly from trivial cases. — [search summary of Kim & Schuster context](https://api.deepai.org/publication/entity-tracking-in-language-models)
- EWoK (world knowledge / pragmatics / commonsense, minimal-pair style) is at or near chance (50.7 baseline, max 58.4) for all 10 M-word BabyLM 2024 models. — [Findings of BabyLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05149)
- BabyLM 2023 also used MSGS (linguistic vs surface generalisation) and an optional age-of-acquisition task alongside BLiMP and (Super)GLUE. — [Findings of BabyLM 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08165)

### Inferences
- Concrete probe designs that follow the published implicit format and fit a 64-token context: (a) name recall: a 40-token story introducing one named character, then "…said" / "…smiled." with the candidate set {introduced name, a distractor name of the same gender frequent in training}; score P(correct) − P(distractor) in logits or accuracy = fraction with correct > distractor; (b) pronoun gender: introduce "Lily"/"Tom" then score "she" vs "he" at the first pronoun slot; (c) object permanence / who-has-what: "Tom had a red ball. He gave it to Lily. Now the ball belongs to" → {Lily, Tom}; (d) counting objects: "Tom had two apples and found one more. Now he had" → {three, two, four} (note this overlaps the arithmetic skill and is the most likely place for a tool-use vs self-compute difference); (e) location tracking exactly as in the sub-billion paper but with story vocabulary and ≤3 moves. Keep recency balanced (vary which mention is most recent) as that paper does, and include a pseudoword/novel-name control to separate tracking from lexical priors.
- Expected signal: Pythia-70M at 53.5 % on multi-move location tracking suggests a 1.4 M story model will be near chance on anything beyond 1–2 moves; the two-candidate name-recall and gender-agreement tasks are the ones likely to be well above chance at this size (TinyEvals already observes pronoun resolution at 1 M), and so are the most sensitive instruments for "freed capacity".
- Generation-based scoring is a bad idea at this scale: even 160 M–1 B base models were at or below chance in the explicit (generate-the-location) format, so use log-probability comparison only.

### Gaps
- No paper publishes name-recall, pronoun-gender, object-permanence or counting probes with numbers for models under 70 M parameters; the entity-tracking paper's smallest model is Pythia-70M and it does not report 160 M/410 M figures in the extracted text.
- No "language models as world models" style probe (e.g., linear decoding of character state) has been run on TinyStories-scale models in any source found.

## KQ4. Held-out perplexity broken down by category (dialogue vs narration, rare vs common words, openings vs endings)

### Takeaway
Per-word/per-token loss decomposition by frequency is an established analysis (Chung & Kim, NeurIPS 2025) and shows that capacity gains land almost entirely on the ~2,500 most frequent words, so a frequency-binned held-out loss is the most sensitive "finer" language measure; dialogue-vs-narration and opening-vs-ending breakdowns have no published precedent for small models but are trivial to compute from token masks.

### Cited Findings
- Word-level loss decomposition: enlarging the vocabulary (24 K→196 K, fixed data/compute) lowers cross-entropy "almost exclusively by lowering uncertainty on the ~2,500 most frequent words" while loss on the rare tail rises; scaling model size with a fixed tokenizer gives the same frequent-word benefit (per-word loss on the 2,500 most frequent FineWeb-Edu words: 4.48 nats at 160 M → 3.76 at 1 B → 3.38 at 6.9 B) and, unlike vocabulary scaling, does not inflate rare-token loss, whose share of total loss shrinks; frequent words cover ~75–80 % of downstream tokens (versions disagree). — [Exploiting Vocabulary Frequency Imbalance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15390)
- TinyStories: GPT-Eval grades rise monotonically as evaluation loss falls, but the paper reports no category-split loss. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- LongTail-Swap: BabyLM metrics "concentrate on the head of the word distribution"; rare-word acceptability is poor and separates architectures more than head-of-distribution tests. — [LongTail-Swap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04268)
- SimpleStories reports judge-free corpus-level statistics (compression ratio, Self-BLEU, n-gram diversity, POS-template rate, top-4-gram share) as proxies for repetitiveness and formulaicness. — [SimpleStories paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09184)
- Induction-heads paper defines an in-context-learning score as loss at token 500 minus loss at token 50 (any choice of indices gives the same conclusions), i.e., a position-split loss. — [In-context Learning and Induction Heads](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/index.html)

### Inferences
- Recommended splits for the user's held-out stories, each a masked mean of per-token NLL: (1) story-only tokens vs arithmetic-problem tokens vs tool-call tokens (the primary comparison: does the delegating model lose less on story tokens?); (2) word-frequency bins (e.g., top-100, 100–1,000, 1,000–4,000 by training count) following Chung & Kim; (3) inside-quotes vs outside-quotes tokens (dialogue vs narration) using quotation marks as a mask; (4) by position in context (tokens 1–16 vs 48–64) as a 64-token analogue of the ICL score; (5) first sentence vs last sentence of a story. Content words and rare-bin tokens are where models at this scale differ most, so bins (2) and (3) are more likely than overall perplexity to reveal a difference.
- Because the user's models see 64 tokens, the ICL score should be "loss at positions 48–64 minus loss at positions 8–16"; a model with working induction/copying will show a larger drop.

### Gaps
- No source reports dialogue-vs-narration or opening-vs-ending perplexity for any small story model; the recommendation above is an inference, not a precedent.
- Chung & Kim's smallest model is 160 M and their data is FineWeb-Edu, so the exact bin boundaries for a 4,000-word vocabulary are untested.

## KQ5. Other cheap skills: induction/copying, repeat-after-me, sequence completion, rhyme/alliteration, instruction following in story form, few-shot pattern completion

### Takeaway
Induction and copying have a precise, CPU-cheap operational test (prefix-matching and copying scores on repeated random-token sequences; ICL score as late-minus-early loss) and are known to be absent in 1-layer models and to emerge as a phase change in ≥2-layer models; rhyme/alliteration have only minimal-pair precedents at large scale (BPoMP limericks, PhonologyBench), and few-shot pattern completion at ≤10 M params is only documented for arithmetic (Lee et al. 2023).

### Cited Findings
- Induction head definition: on a repeated random token sequence, a head that (i) attends back to the token that followed the previous occurrence of the current token (prefix matching) and (ii) raises the logit of the attended token (copying); the two measurements form a continuum; induction heads are "implemented by a circuit consisting of a pair of attention heads in different layers" and do not occur in 1-layer models; the phase change is visible as a bump in training loss in models with more than one layer, after which loss keeps improving past token 50 of the context; the ICL score = loss at token 500 minus loss at token 50; the study covers 34 transformers and >50,000 head ablations. — [In-context Learning and Induction Heads](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/index.html)
- Concrete induction-score recipe (Yin & Steinhardt 2025): sample random tokens r_1…r_50, repeat them, score each head by attention from positions in the second copy to the token following the match in the first copy, average over 1,000 random sequences, normalise by total attention mass to [0, 1]; implemented in `find_induction_heads.py`; TransformerLens prepends BOS by default, which shifts indices. — [Which Attention Heads Matter for ICL?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.14010); [icl-heads repo](https://github.com/kayoyin/icl-heads)
- TinyEvals observed "repeated tokens" and "repeated multi-token names (possibly induction)" among the 1 M TinyStories model's capabilities, and the TinyStories interpretability section finds only distance-based and semantic heads in a 1-layer model (no induction heads discussed). — [TinyEvals](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/aHE6rejJEkWspwnKu/aisc-project-tinyevals); [TinyStories HTML §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Few-shot arithmetic at ~10 M params: NanoGPT (6 layers, 6 heads, d = 384, ~10.6 M) trained on Shakespeare (1,115,394 tokens, character-level vocab 80) interleaved with 10,000 plain and 3,000 scratchpad addition examples; accuracy measured zero-shot and with 1/2/3-shot exemplars; few-shot exemplars help, text prompts perform like zero-shot, plain addition is "almost perfect with 40,000 train samples"; text prompts hurt when the model is trained on addition only. — [Teaching Arithmetic to Small Transformers](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03381v1)
- Instruction following in story form: TinyStories-Instruct (word list / required sentence / features / summary prefixes), scored by GPT-4 for instruction consistency and plot; one layer struggles, two layers suffice for some; depends more on depth. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Rhyme minimal pairs: BPoMP (2021) adapts BLiMP-style minimal pairs to poetry with 10,000 pairs of original vs corrupted limericks (AABBA), scored by whether the intact limerick gets higher probability (surfaced via search; URL not verified, see Gaps). PhonologyBench (2024) tests grapheme-to-phoneme, syllable counting and rhyme-word generation for LLMs; no model consistently wins. — [PhonologyBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.02456v1)
- BabyLM phonology work uses minimal pairs of words vs pseudowords as a lexical-decision probe and mentions rhyme-prediction probing tasks. — [BabyLM's First Words](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.03338)

### Inferences
- Copy/induction suite for a 64-token model: (a) repeated random-token test with sequence length 30 repeated once (fits 64 tokens): report mean NLL on the second copy (perfect induction → near 0) and the per-head prefix-matching score; (b) "repeat-after-me" with real story words: a sequence of 8–12 random vocabulary words followed by the same words, NLL on the repeat; (c) name-copying: a novel two-token name introduced once, score P(second token | first token) at its second mention. All are one forward pass per item, deterministic, and 1,000 items run in seconds on CPU. Expect 1-layer models to fail (a) and (c) outright and ≥2-layer models to show a sharp transition during training, which also gives a cheap "did the phase change happen" diagnostic for the 7,000-step runs.
- Rhyme/alliteration is a poor test for a 4,000-word story model: the lexicon rarely rhymes systematically and the training data contains almost no verse, so a BPoMP-style corrupted-rhyme pair would mostly measure n-gram familiarity; if desired, build alliteration pairs from story names ("Peter picked purple plums" vs mixed onsets) and expect near-chance results.
- Few-shot pattern completion in story form (e.g., "The cat says meow. The dog says woof. The cow says" with 2–3 in-context examples) is a direct analogue of Lee et al.'s few-shot test and of the ICL score, and is where a working induction circuit should pay off.

### Gaps
- No source reports induction-head emergence or ICL scores for models under ~1 M–10 M parameters trained on stories; the phase-change token count (commonly quoted as a few billion tokens for Pile-trained models) could not be verified from the fetched text and may not transfer to a 7,000-step CPU run.
- The BPoMP paper's URL was not confirmed by the search tool (only an author page surfaced); no rhyme/alliteration evaluation exists for tiny models.
- Lee et al.'s Shakespeare-plus-addition text-perplexity numbers (Appendix C figure) were not extractable from the HTML text.

## KQ6. Data-mixture evidence at small scale: does math, code or structured data help or hurt language metrics?

### Takeaway
At 18 M–100 M parameters on 10 M words, narrow synthetic story data (TinyStories) lowers BLiMP by 3–6 points relative to a mixed natural corpus and pulls mixtures toward the TinyStories-only result, while at 374 M replacing language data with code hurts English passivization, morphology and general-knowledge tasks but helps compositional/structured-output generalisation and multi-digit arithmetic; at 470 M–2.8 B a ~25 % code share is net positive for NL reasoning and 100 % code costs 18 % of it. No study mixes arithmetic into story training at 1–10 M parameters and measures language skills, so the user's experiment has no direct precedent.

### Cited Findings
- "What Should Baby Models Read?" (Nov 2024): GPT2-18M / 44M / 97M (TinyStories-style architectures; 44 M = hidden 768, 2 layers, 8 heads) and Llama-20M trained 4 epochs on 10 M words of CHILDES, Gutenberg, the BabyLM mix, or TinyStories. BLiMP-filtered / BLiMP-Supplement / EWoK: GPT2-18M Mix 63.7 / 55.9 / 49.7 vs TinyStories 57.5 / 55.2 / 50.7; GPT2-44M Mix 65.6 / 58.2 / 50.4 vs TinyStories 57.1 / 52.8 / 50.4; GPT2-97M Mix 66.0 / 58.0 / 50.6 vs TinyStories 59.1 / 54.6 / 50.3; Llama-20M Mix 62.8 / 56.6 / 50.2 vs TinyStories 51.1 / 46.7 / 49.8. GLUE zero-shot macro: TinyStories best at 18 M (44.35) and 44 M (44.58). Conclusions: small models do best on Mix, larger on Gutenberg; CHILDES and TinyStories underperform on BLiMP/EWoK at all sizes, plausibly because those benchmarks test constructions absent from the data. — [What Should Baby Models Read?](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06672)
- BERTtime Stories (BabyLM 2024): LTG-BERT 24 M (12 layers, hidden 384) on 10 M words: BabyLM-only BLiMP 62.8 / Supp 63.7 / EWoK 51.2 / GLUE 71.0; TinyStories-only 59.8 / 54.2 / 52.2 / 67.0; 5 M+5 M 62.6 / 60.7 / 51.5 / 71.2; plus balanced nucleus-1 generations 63.2 / 59.3 / 50.4 / 71.1. At 100 M (98 M model): BabyLM-only 64.0 / 67.6 / 47.3 / 74.0; TinyStories-only 61.2 / 63.2 / 48.0 / 70.6; 50 M+50 M 65.5 / 65.6 / 47.2 / 71.0; +greedy generations 66.6 / 63.3 / 49.7 / 71.8. Conclusion: synthetic story data "can occasionally offer modest gains, but overall have a negative influence on linguistic understanding"; more TinyStories pulls results toward the TinyStories-only numbers. — [BERTtime Stories](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15365)
- "Are BabyLMs Second Language Learners?": adding grammar-focused data helped BLiMP only slightly and hurt other metrics; GPT-3.5-generated text was less useful for BLiMP than grammar-book text because it looked too natural for BLiMP's deliberately unnatural pairs. — [Are BabyLMs Second Language Learners?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.21254)
- BabyLM 2024 aggregate: dataset creation/augmentation had a positive effect on average text score (β = 4.8, p < 0.05); one team extended the TinyStories approach with GPT-4 stories in a 3–4-year-old vocabulary; the findings paper does not isolate any single data choice's BLiMP effect. — [Findings of BabyLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05149)
- Code at 374 M params, 132 B language tokens, 5 seeds per mixture, competitive (code replaces text, 0–90 %) and additive (code added, 0–50 %) settings: code raises COGS/COGS-vf compositional generalisation (slopes 0.147 competitive, 0.165 additive on structural generalisation) and BigBench multi-digit arithmetic (additive slopes 0.172 for 1-digit to 0.397 for 5-digit; competitive peaks at 40–50 % code), but lowers English Passivization (−0.416 competitive, −0.263/−0.193 additive), Common Morpheme (−0.093 / −0.049), Fantasy Reasoning (−0.047 / −0.062), General Knowledge (−0.084 / −0.097); well-formedness of outputs barely changes; no BLiMP or perplexity reported; no model under 374 M. — [How Does Code Pretraining Affect LM Task Performance?](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04556)
- "To Code, or Not To Code?" (470 M and 2.8 B): balanced→text vs text-only gives +8.2 % relative NL reasoning, +4.2 % world knowledge, +6.6 % generative win-rate, ~12× code; ~25 % code is the best average, with +3.4 % NL reasoning over 0 % code; 100 % code costs −18.3 % NL reasoning and −86 % world knowledge; high-quality synthetic code adds +9 % NL reasoning over web code; cooldown with code +3.6 % NL reasoning, +10.1 % world knowledge; at 470 M only benchmarks above chance were kept; the code/NL trade-off grew with scale. — [To Code, or Not To Code?](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10914)
- Data-constrained scaling (NeurIPS 2023): mixing Python code into natural-language training at 10 mixing rates "is able to provide a 2× increase in effective tokens" even when only NL tasks are evaluated; repeating data up to ~4 epochs costs almost nothing. — [Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264)
- phi-1 (1.3 B, code-only): ~6 B tokens of filtered web code plus ~1 B tokens of GPT-3.5 textbooks/exercises; HumanEval 50.6 %, MBPP 55.5 %; phi-1-small (350 M) reaches 45 % HumanEval; the abstract claims "surprising emergent properties" after exercise fine-tuning without attributing them to textbook data. — [phi-1 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11644)
- phi-1.5 (1.3 B): natural-language performance comparable to models 5× larger using mostly synthetic textbook-style data (~20 B tokens) plus phi-1's code data; shows typical LLM traits including hallucination; secondary sources disagree on the exact mix. — [phi-1.5 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05463v1)
- SmolLM2 (2025): small models are more sensitive to noisy training data and the code/math share needs careful tuning (ablations at 350 B tokens scored with HumanEval/MultiPL-E). — [SmolLM2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02737)
- Arithmetic mixed with text at ~10 M: Lee et al. interleave addition examples with Shakespeare and vary the counts; the few-shot and text-prompt behaviour depends on the combined data; text-perplexity-vs-ratio curves exist only as appendix figures. — [Teaching Arithmetic to Small Transformers](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03381v1)
- "Positional Description Matters": naively mixing arithmetic with natural language "we don't see a significant boost in accuracy" on arithmetic because arithmetic embedded in text breaks the positional pattern; fixes via data format and position embeddings. — [Positional Description Matters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14737)
- Entity tracking surfaced zero-shot only in code-pretrained GPT-3.5 variants (Kim & Schuster 2023). — [Entity Tracking in Language Models](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.213)

### Inferences
- The strongest small-scale lesson is about coverage, not about math per se: a benchmark only moves with training data that contains the tested constructions (TinyStories → low BLiMP; grammar-book text → BLiMP up but other metrics down). For the user, this means the comparison should be on story-vocabulary tests built from the same distribution, and any arithmetic-share change should be interpreted first as a change in how many story tokens the model saw.
- The code evidence at 374 M says structured data trades surface-linguistic skill (passivization, morphemes) for structured/compositional skill and arithmetic; the user's "calculator" variant removes the need to learn digit manipulation, which by analogy to the competitive setting should return capacity to the morphology/agreement end of the spectrum, which is exactly what KQ2 minimal pairs and KQ4 frequency-binned loss measure.
- The 25 %-code optimum (470 M–2.8 B) and the 2× effective-token result are compute-rich regimes; at 1.4 M params and 7,000 CPU steps the model is capacity-limited rather than data-limited, so expect the competitive (replacement) results, where structured data hurts linguistic tasks, to be the better analogy than the additive ones.

### Gaps
- No study trains 0.5–10 M models on stories plus arithmetic word problems and measures grammar/consistency; the only ≤10 M mixed run (Lee et al.) measures arithmetic accuracy and leaves text perplexity in unextracted appendix figures.
- No source tests tool-call/delegation formats as training data, so the "freed capacity" hypothesis has no published precedent at any scale found here.
- Phi-1/phi-1.5 give no controlled ablation of code vs language ability at small scale; the retrieved claims are at 350 M–1.3 B and partly from secondary sources.

## KQ7. Evaluation harnesses, tooling for tiny models with custom tokenizers, and getting stable numbers

### Takeaway
The BabyLM pipelines (2024: lm-evaluation-harness fork with `backend="causal"` for custom models; 2025: from-scratch scripts taking an architecture flag) run BLiMP/EWoK/GLUE zero-shot by comparing sentence log-probabilities, which is exactly the loop a 4,000-word custom model needs; stable numbers come from ≥500–1,000 items per test, bootstrap standard errors, several training seeds (5 per mixture in the code-mixing study), and reporting ties and frequency-stratified subsets.

### Cited Findings
- BabyLM 2024 pipeline is a fork of EleutherAI's lm-evaluation-harness; run `./eval_blimp.sh <path_to_model>`; custom architectures need `backend="mlm"` or `backend="causal"` in `--model_args`. — [evaluation-pipeline-2024](https://github.com/babylm/evaluation-pipeline-2024)
- BabyLM 2025 pipeline was implemented from scratch; text-only script `./eval_zero_shot.sh <path_to_model> <architecture (causal/mntp/mlm/enc_dec_mask/enc_dec_prefix)>`; BLiMP, EWoK and GLUE remain included; BLiMP is zero-shot (higher probability to the grammatical member). — [evaluation-pipeline-2025](https://github.com/babylm/evaluation-pipeline-2025)
- lm-eval reports a bootstrap standard error of the mean; `--limit` subsamples items (quick tests only). — [lm-eval quickstart](https://lm-evaluation-harness.readthedocs.io/getting_started/quickstart/)
- The harness authors note the bootstrap CI answers whether the benchmark's questions are a sample from a distribution of questions, not run-to-run variance. — [Lessons from the Trenches](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.14782)
- evalci: on a few-thousand-item benchmark a 2-point accuracy gap "is frequently within the noise implied by the sample size alone"; it reads lm-eval output. — [evalci](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04429)
- Tie artifact and tokenizer sensitivity: identical logits for both members of a pair earn full credit in the BabyLM pipeline; results are highly sensitive to tokenization. — [Elementary Baselines for BabyLM](https://aclanthology.org/2025.babylm-main.16/)
- Precedents for sample sizes: TinyStories ≈50 prompts × 10 completions = 500 graded stories per model — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2); SimpleStories N = 200 stories with 99.9 % CIs — [SimpleStories](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09184); BLiMP 1,000 pairs per paradigm — [BLiMP](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.00582); code-mixing study 5 random seeds per mixture — [Code pretraining study](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04556); induction score averaged over 1,000 random sequences — [Which Attention Heads Matter](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.14010); entity-tracking study varied focus position F0–F3 and complexity C1–C5 as controls — [Entity tracking](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18083)
- delphi provides a reproducible train-tokenizer → tokenize → train pipeline with a 4,096-token BPE and HF-compatible checkpoints, which the BabyLM pipeline can consume as a CausalLM. — [delphi](https://github.com/delphi-suite/delphi)

### Inferences
- Practical stack: export the 1.4 M model as an HF `CausalLM` with the 4,000-word tokenizer (delphi's pattern), write custom JSONL minimal pairs and (prompt, correct, wrong) probe files, and either plug them into the 2024 lm-eval fork as new tasks or run a 20-line PyTorch scorer; everything is a batch of forward passes over ≤64-token inputs, well under a minute per 1,000 items on CPU.
- Power: binomial SE ≈ sqrt(p(1−p)/n) gives ±1.4 pp at n = 1,000 and p = 0.75, ±3.1 pp at n = 200; a loss difference of 0.01 nats on 100 k held-out tokens is resolvable, but grading-style differences under ~0.5 points (TinyStories scale) are not with 200–500 stories. Train ≥3 seeds per condition and report per-seed numbers; count ties as 0.5 and report the tie rate; stratify agreement pairs and loss by training frequency of the critical word.

### Gaps
- No official statement of a minimum item count for a reliable lm-eval stderr was found; the figures above are inferences from the binomial formula and the evalci remark.
- Whether the 2024 lm-eval fork accepts a non-HF tokenizer directly was not verified beyond the README's `backend` note.

## Consolidated catalogue of candidate measures (summary for the report writer)

### Takeaway
Nine measure families are cheap enough for a 1.4 M model on a laptop; the three with both precedent and expected sensitivity at this size are frequency-binned held-out story loss, in-vocabulary agreement minimal pairs, and two-candidate name/pronoun tracking probes, with repeated-sequence induction tests as a structural diagnostic and GPT-4o-mini-style grading only as an optional check.

### Cited Findings
- Held-out loss split by token class/frequency/position: measures fluency and lexical knowledge; scored as masked mean NLL; cost ≈ one pass over the validation set; precedent Chung & Kim word-level decomposition and the ICL score; typical result: gains concentrate in the ~2,500 most frequent words. — [Chung & Kim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15390); [Induction heads](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/index.html)
- Agreement minimal pairs from the story vocabulary: measures morphosyntax; scored as fraction of pairs where the grammatical sentence has higher summed log-prob (ties counted against); cost 1,000 forward passes per paradigm; precedent BLiMP/BabyLM; typical: 0.63–0.70 for 10 M-word causal baselines, 0.80–0.81 best; agreement paradigms easiest. — [BLiMP](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.00582); [BabyLM 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08165); [BabyLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05149)
- Name recall / pronoun gender / who-has-what / location tracking: measures consistency and world tracking; scored by log-prob of correct vs distractor continuation; precedent entity-tracking implicit format and TinyEvals; typical: Pythia-70M 53.5 % on multi-move tracking, pronoun resolution present at 1 M TinyStories. — [Entity tracking](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18083); [TinyEvals](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/aHE6rejJEkWspwnKu/aisc-project-tinyevals)
- Repeated-random-sequence induction test and prefix-matching/copying scores: measures in-context copying; scored by NLL on the second copy and per-head attention to the correct offset; precedent Olsson et al. 2022 and Yin & Steinhardt 2025; typical: absent in 1-layer models, phase change in ≥2 layers. — [Induction heads](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/index.html); [Which Attention Heads Matter](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.14010)
- Few-shot pattern completion in story form: measures in-context learning; scored as accuracy on the final slot; precedent Lee et al. few-shot addition at 10.6 M. — [Teaching Arithmetic](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03381v1)
- Instruction-in-story following (word list / required sentence / feature prefix): measures controllability; precedent TinyStories-Instruct (needs ≥2 layers); scored by GPT-4 originally, or by a string check for word-list/required-sentence inclusion. — [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Judge-free diversity/formulaicness statistics (Self-BLEU, n-gram diversity, template rate, top-4-gram share): proxies for creativity; precedent SimpleStories. — [SimpleStories](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09184)
- Small-judge grading (GPT-4o-mini, N = 200, 0–100 on grammar/coherence/quality/originality): precedent SimpleStories; TinyStories' 1–10 GPT-4 grading is the reference. — [SimpleStories](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09184); [TinyStories HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07759v2)
- Rhyme/alliteration minimal pairs: precedent BPoMP limericks / PhonologyBench at LLM scale only. — [PhonologyBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.02456v1)

### Inferences
- Order of expected sensitivity for the delegating-vs-computing comparison at 1.4 M: (1) story-token-only loss and its rare-word bin, (2) agreement minimal pairs, (3) name/pronoun tracking, (4) induction/ICL score, (5) judge grading. The arithmetic-adjacent counting probe should be analysed separately because it is the one place where the self-computing model is expected to win.

### Gaps
- Absolute expected values for every measure at 0.5–10 M parameters are unpublished; the literature's smallest quantitative points are 1.25 M–35 M (SimpleStories, judge scores), 18 M (BLiMP 57–64), 70 M (entity tracking 53.5 %), and 10.6 M (few-shot addition).
